Order types and the life of an order

Market, limit, stop and stop-limit orders explained with illustrative numbers, plus the life of an order from creation to fill or cancel, partial fills, rejections and slippage.

Reading level

An order is an instruction to a broker: buy or sell this much, on these terms. Different order types let you state different terms. This part explains the common ones with plain pictures and illustrative numbers, follows an order through its life, and then shows how trading software models that life. Each section has an Expert view. This is general education about how orders work, not advice about which to use.

The four common order types

In plain words

Think of buying concert tickets. A market order is "I want a ticket straight away, whatever it costs". A limit order is "I will pay at most 100, and I will wait". A stop order is "if the price reaches 95, act". A stop-limit is "if the price reaches 95, act, but never pay more than 96".

Illustrative numbers

All prices below are illustrative. Suppose a share is priced at 100, with a best ask of 100.10 and a best bid of 99.90.

Order typeWhat it isWhen it triggersThe main risk
Market (buying)A purchase at the best available prices, straight awayImmediatelyThe fill price may differ from the price you saw
Limit (buying) at 99.80A purchase only at 99.80 or lowerWaits in the book until a seller accepts 99.80It may never be filled
Stop (selling) at 95A sale that activates if the price falls to 95When trading reaches the trigger of 95It becomes a market order, so the price can be worse than 95
Stop-limit (selling), trigger 95, limit 94A sale at 94 or better, activated once 95 is reachedWhen trading reaches 95, then it rests as a limit orderThe price may jump past 94 and nothing is sold
Expert viewTriggers and conditions

A stop trigger is usually tested against a particular price (for example the last traded price), and brokers and venues differ in which one, and in whether the stop lives at the broker or the venue. A stop order is a conditional order: until triggered it is not in the order book at all. A stop-limit gives price control after the trigger, but, like any limit order, offers no assurance of execution. Always read the exact behaviour in your broker's own documentation.

The life of an order

Along the way things can go differently: the order may be rejected, only partly filled, or cancelled before it is completed.

Partial fills

An order for 500 may find only 200 waiting at its price. The first 200 trade, and the other 300 remain open (or are cancelled, depending on the terms).

Expert viewPartial fill arithmetic

Suppose an order to buy 500 fills in two pieces: 200 at 100.10 and 300 at 100.20 (illustrative). Total cost is 200 x 100.10 + 300 x 100.20 = 20,020 + 30,060 = 50,080. The average fill price is 50,080 / 500 = 100.16. Software tracks the cumulative quantity filled, the remaining quantity, and the average price, and the remaining quantity must always equal ordered minus filled.

Why orders get rejected

A rejection is usually a good thing for the system: a clear refusal is better than a silent mistake.

Expert viewRisk checks before an order leaves

Well-designed systems run pre-trade checks before an order is sent: maximum order size, maximum price distance from the market, available funds, limits on total exposure, and duplicate detection. Checks run in a fixed order and produce a specific reason code, so a rejection can always be explained and logged.

Slippage

Slippage is the gap between the price you expected and the price you actually got.

In plain words

Like seeing a price tag on a shelf, and finding the price at the till is a little higher because the last cheap items just sold.

Careful

Slippage can be larger when markets are thin, moving fast, or when an order is large. It can be unfavourable, and sometimes favourable, but it cannot be predicted in advance.

Time in force

Time in force says how long an order lives. In general terms, a day order expires at the end of the trading session, and a good until cancelled order stays until it is filled or you withdraw it. Venues and brokers offer different options and use different names, so check what the one you use calls them.

A common misunderstanding is that a limit order always gets filled eventually. In fact a limit order is filled only if the market reaches its price, and it may expire, or stay open for a long time, without any fill. Another is that a day order and a good until cancelled order differ only in name: they differ in how long they stay exposed to the market, including during sudden moves.

An order is a small state machine

Software that handles orders models each one as a state machine: a fixed set of states, and only certain moves between them. Every change is recorded as an event, so the history of an order can be replayed later. This is the idea behind the Trading platform section on the home page.

Expert viewState machine design

Good designs follow a few rules. Valid transitions only: a filled order can never become open again, and any message implying that is rejected and flagged. Idempotent handling: the same message can arrive twice, so processing it again must change nothing; a unique event identifier makes duplicates easy to detect. Reconciliation with the venue: periodically compare your own record of every open order and fill with the venue's, and investigate any difference. Events are stored in order, so the current state is simply the result of replaying them. A useful test of a design is to ask whether the whole history of a day could be rebuilt from the event log alone, and whether two copies built that way would agree. Where they do not, there is a bug or a missing event, and the difference is the thing to investigate.

An order is not a click, it is a life with a beginning, a middle and an end.

Idempotent
Handling the same message twice has the same effect as handling it once.

Frequently asked questions

Which order type is safest?

None is safe in every case. Each exchanges one kind of certainty for another, such as certainty of a fill versus certainty of price.

Can a stop order lose more than the trigger price suggests?

Yes. Once triggered it may become a market order, and the fill can be worse than the trigger.

What happens to the unfilled part of an order?

It stays open, expires, or is cancelled, depending on the order terms.

Why might my order be rejected?

Typical reasons are an invalid size, a price outside limits, insufficient funds or margin, or a closed market.

Careful

Educational content, not investment advice. Trading, especially with leverage and options, can lose more than you expect.
