How ranking and recommendations work

A general, plain-language look at scoring, similarity, "people who liked this also liked that", the idea behind PageRank, and the cautions around bias, feedback loops and privacy.

Reading level

Lists of films, search results, suggested songs, "you might also like": all of these are ranked lists. Someone, or something, decided what comes first. This part explains the general ideas behind ranking and recommendation in plain words. It describes standard techniques only. It makes no claims about how any particular company's system works, because those details are private and change over time. Each section has a plain layer and an Expert view.

Step one: score, then sort

In plain words

Imagine choosing a restaurant. You might care about taste, price and distance. You give each a weight (taste matters most), add them up, and the highest total wins. A ranking system does the same thing with many more signals.

The score is often a weighted sum: each signal is multiplied by a weight that says how much it matters, and the results are added. Sorting is then a solved problem (see the sorting part of this series).

Expert viewScores in practice

The weights can be set by hand or learned from data (see the machine learning part). Scores are usually only meaningful for comparison: a score of 0.8 does not mean 80 percent of anything. Real systems often work in two stages: a cheap method picks a few hundred candidates from millions, then a more expensive model ranks only those. Only the top of the list is ever seen, so small differences near the top matter most.

Similarity: taste as numbers

In plain words

Suppose you and a friend rate five films from 1 to 5. If your ratings move together, you have similar taste. Describe each person as a list of numbers, and "similar" becomes "close together".

The same trick works for items: describe a film by numbers (how much action, how much comedy) and find films whose numbers are near a film you liked.

Expert viewMeasuring closeness

Each person or item becomes a vector, a list of numbers. Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors: it is 1 when they point the same way, 0 when they are unrelated (at right angles), and negative when they point in opposite directions. It ignores length, so someone who rates everything high and someone who rates everything low can still match in direction. Euclidean distance, the straight-line gap, is another common choice. With many items, vectors are mostly empty (most people rate few films), so systems store only the non-empty entries.

People who liked this also liked that

In plain words

A shop notices that many people who buy tea also buy a teapot. When you pick tea, it suggests a teapot. Nobody needed to understand tea. The pattern came from counting.

Expert viewCollaborative and content-based

This counting idea is part of collaborative filtering: use the behaviour of many users to predict one user's taste, without looking at what the items are. Content-based filtering does the opposite: recommend items whose own features resemble things you liked. A popular collaborative approach, matrix factorisation, imagines a big table of users against items with many blanks, and finds a small set of hidden "taste dimensions" for every user and item such that combining them approximately fills in the table. The blanks are then predicted. Hybrids combine both families.

The PageRank idea

In plain words

Which web page is important? One answer: a page is important if important pages link to it. A link is a vote, and votes from important pages count for more. This sounds circular, but it can be solved.

The usual picture is a random surfer who keeps clicking links at random. Over a very long time, the pages the surfer visits most often are the ones with the highest importance.

Expert viewDamping and convergence

In the standard PageRank formulation, at each step the surfer follows a random link with probability d (the damping factor, commonly chosen near 0.85 in descriptions of the method) and otherwise jumps to a random page. The jump handles pages with no outgoing links and prevents the surfer being trapped in a small cluster. The scores are the steady-state probabilities of this process. They can be found by power iteration: start with equal scores and repeatedly apply the update, which converges, so the changes shrink until they are negligible. The same idea applies to any network of links, not just web pages.

When the system has nothing to go on

Feedback loops and bubbles

Popular items get seen, so they get more clicks, so they become more popular. Newer or niche items may never get a fair chance. If you are shown only what resembles your past choices, your view can narrow: a filter bubble.

Expert viewBias, explainability and privacy

Popularity bias is the tendency to over-recommend what is already popular. Training data reflects past behaviour, so it can carry human and historical bias forward, and groups that are under-represented in the data may be served worse. Mitigations include adding exploration (occasionally showing less certain items), diversifying results, and auditing outcomes by group.

Explainability helps users and reviewers: "recommended because you watched X" is easier to check than an unexplained score. Recommenders also use personal data, so data minimisation, clear consent and letting users reset or remove their history are good practice. Privacy law varies by place and I do not give legal advice here.

Is it any good?

Expert viewEvaluation traps

Offline evaluation uses historical data and standard measures such as precision at k (how many of the top k were relevant) and ranking quality measures that reward putting relevant items first. Its weakness is that the history was itself shaped by an earlier system. Online evaluation measures real behaviour but needs care: short-term clicks can reward attention-grabbing items over useful ones, so teams also track longer-term signals such as satisfaction, returns and complaints. No single number captures "good".

Glossary
Ranking
Putting items in order by a score.
Signal
A measurable clue used in a score, such as a rating.
Vector
A list of numbers that describes an item or a person.
Cosine similarity
A measure of the angle between two vectors; smaller angle, more similar.
Collaborative filtering
Predicting taste from the behaviour of many users.
PageRank
A method that scores a page by the importance of the pages linking to it.
Cold start
Having too little data on a new user or item.
Filter bubble
A narrowed view caused by seeing only items similar to past choices.

Frequently asked questions

Does a recommender understand what I like?

No. It finds patterns in numbers, such as ratings and clicks, and predicts from them.

Why do I keep seeing similar things?

Systems often favour what resembles past choices, which can narrow results unless variety is added on purpose.

What is the damping factor in PageRank?

The probability that the random surfer follows a link rather than jumping to a random page.

How can a recommender be unfair?

If its data under-represents some people, or reflects past bias, it may serve them worse unless it is checked.

Key takeaways

  • Ranking usually means scoring items and sorting by the score
  • Similarity turns taste into numbers, and closer numbers mean more alike
  • Recommenders can feed on themselves, so they need careful evaluation beyond clicks
