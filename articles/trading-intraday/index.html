Intraday trading explained

What intraday trading means, how square-off, leverage and margin work, why costs and time pressure matter, and a plain statement of the risks. General education, not advice.

Reading level

Intraday trading is one of the most talked-about styles of trading, and one of the least understood. This part explains what the words mean, how leverage and costs work, and what the risks are, in plain language with illustrative numbers. Each section also has an Expert view. It is general education only and does not suggest that anyone should or should not trade.

What intraday means

An intraday position is opened and closed within the same trading day. Nothing is held overnight. A position simply means an amount of something you currently own or owe through trading.

In plain words

Compare renting a stall at a market for one day with buying a shop. The stall-holder must pack up by evening and cannot leave stock lying around. Intraday positions are like the stall: they are meant to be cleared before the day finishes.

Square-off

Square-off means closing an open position by doing the opposite trade. If you bought, you sell the same quantity, and the position is flat again. In general terms, a broker may close positions that are still open at the end of the session automatically, whether or not you wanted to, and it may charge a fee for doing so. Rules and timings vary between brokers and markets, so the exact policy is something to read in the broker's own terms.

Careful

An automatic close happens at whatever price is available at that moment, which may be a poor one if the market is thin or moving fast.

Leverage and margin

Leverage means controlling a larger position than your own money would normally allow. The broker asks for a deposit called margin, which acts as security, and lends the rest.

In plain words

It is like putting down a small deposit on a large purchase. If the thing rises in value, you gain on the whole amount while having paid only the deposit. If it falls, you lose on the whole amount too, and the deposit is the first thing to disappear.

Real example

Illustrative loss. A position of 10,000 is opened with a deposit of 1,000. The price falls by 5 percent, so the position is now worth 9,500, a loss of 500. That is half of the deposit, from a 5 percent move. If it falls by 10 percent, the loss is 1,000: the whole deposit. If it falls further, the loss can exceed the deposit.

Gains work in the mirror-image way, but there is no reason to expect the price to move in a particular direction, and losses are equally likely to be magnified.

Expert viewMargin and mark-to-market

Mark-to-market means revaluing open positions at current prices, usually throughout the day, so that gains and losses are reflected in the account straight away rather than only when you close. Brokers set initial margin to open a position and a lower maintenance margin level. If losses push the account's equity below the required level, the broker issues a margin call, asking for more funds, or may close positions without waiting. Required margin can change quickly when markets become more volatile, so a position that was comfortable can suddenly need more security.

Why leverage raises the risk of ruin

Expert viewRisk of ruin

Risk of ruin is the chance of losing so much that you cannot continue. Leverage raises it because the same price move takes a bigger share of your capital, so fewer adverse moves are needed to reach a point of no return. It also shrinks the room for ordinary day-to-day fluctuation: a move that would be a small wobble unleveraged can trigger forced closing when leveraged. Losses that exceed the deposit are possible, particularly when prices jump past a level without trading in between, which is called a gap.

Costs add up

Each trade carries these costs whether it ends in a gain or a loss. Trading often means paying them often.

Expert viewTransaction cost drag

Suppose, illustratively, that the total cost of one round trip (buying and selling) is 0.20 on a position of 100, which is 0.2 percent. If you make 5 round trips a day for 20 trading days, that is 100 round trips, and the cost is 100 x 0.2 percent = 20 percent of the position size, before any price movement at all. Prices would have to move favourably by that much just to cover the costs. This is a simple illustration of why frequent trading makes costs matter more, and the real figures depend on the product, the broker and taxes.

Time pressure and emotions

Everything happens fast within a day. Prices move every second, news arrives, and a position must be closed by the end of the session. That pressure can make people act on impulse: closing too early from fear, holding on to avoid admitting a loss, or trading more to try to recover. These reactions are human and common, which is why many people write down rules for themselves in advance.

Careful

Many beginners lose money when they trade short-term. Leverage, costs, time pressure and unfamiliarity all contribute, and losing more than you intended is a real possibility.

A generic trading day

Expert viewExecution quality

Execution quality is how good the fills are compared with what was possible: the size of the spread paid, the amount of slippage, how quickly the order was filled, and how often it was filled at all. It depends on the type of order used, the depth of the book at that moment, the broker's routing and your own order size. Measuring it means comparing each fill with a reference price from the moment of the order, and averaging over many trades rather than judging one.

Questions to ask before you start

This is a checklist for thinking, not a recommendation to trade.

Before asking how much could be gained, ask how much could be lost.

Glossary
Intraday
Opened and closed within one trading day.
Square-off
Closing a position by doing the opposite trade.
Margin
The deposit a broker requires as security for a leveraged position.
Leverage
Controlling a larger position than your own money alone would allow.
Mark-to-market
Revaluing open positions at current prices.
Margin call
A broker's demand for more funds when losses reduce the account's equity.
Slippage
The difference between expected and obtained price.
Gap
A jump in price with no trading in between.

Frequently asked questions

Does intraday mean low risk because nothing is held overnight?

No. Prices can move sharply within a day, and leverage can magnify those moves into large losses.

Can I lose more than I deposited?

With leveraged products it is possible, depending on the product and the broker's terms.

Why do costs matter so much?

They are paid on every trade, so the more often you trade, the more they take from the outcome.

Is intraday trading the same as investing?

No. Investing usually means holding for a long time, while intraday means closing everything the same day.

Careful

Educational content, not investment advice. Trading, especially with leverage and options, can lose more than you expect.

Key takeaways

  • Intraday means positions are opened and closed within one trading day
  • Leverage magnifies losses as much as gains, and costs add up when you trade often
  • Short-term trading is a very different activity from investing
