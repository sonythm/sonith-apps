Greedy, divide and conquer, and backtracking

Three classic problem-solving styles, grab the best now, split the job, or try and back up, with everyday pictures, when each works, when it fails, and how to choose.

  • Divide and conquer splits a problem, solves the pieces and combines them
  • Backtracking tries choices and undoes them, pruning dead ends early

Reading level

Earlier parts showed individual algorithms. This part steps back to three styles of designing one, each with a different attitude to making choices. Greedy commits to whatever looks best right now. Divide and conquer splits the problem into smaller copies. Backtracking tries a choice, and if it leads nowhere, undoes it and tries another. Knowing which style fits a problem is half of solving it. Each section has a plain picture and an Expert view.

Three styles at a glance

In plain words

Greedy is a hungry person at a buffet taking the best-looking dish each time. Divide and conquer is a manager splitting a report between four colleagues and stitching the pieces together. Backtracking is walking a maze with a ball of string: at a dead end, follow the string back to the last fork.

Expert viewA note on how they relate

Dynamic programming (part 6) can be seen as a fourth style that sits between these: like divide and conquer it splits problems, but it stores overlapping answers. Backtracking is a systematic search of a tree of choices, closely related to depth-first search from part 5. Many problems can be attacked with more than one style, and the styles differ in what they guarantee: greedy gives a guarantee only when a special property holds, while backtracking can be made to find an answer or prove none exists, at a possibly large cost.

Greedy: take the best choice now

In plain words

Making change with everyday coins: to pay 68, take the biggest coin that fits, then repeat. It works well, and it is quick to do in your head.

Greedy is fast and simple, but it is only correct when local best choices add up to a global best. That depends on the problem.

Real example

Activity selection. You have many events with start and end times, and can attend only one at a time. To attend the most events, repeatedly choose the event that finishes earliest among those that do not clash with what you have chosen. This simple rule is known to give a largest possible set.

Expert viewWhen can you trust greedy?

Two properties are usually proved: the greedy-choice property (some optimal solution begins with the greedy choice) and optimal substructure (after that choice, the rest is a smaller problem of the same type). The standard method is an exchange argument: take any optimal solution and show you can swap in the greedy choice without making it worse. Well-known correct greedy algorithms include activity selection by earliest finish, Huffman coding, and Dijkstra's algorithm for non-negative weights (part 5). Without such a proof, assume greedy may fail and test it on small cases against a slower exhaustive method.

Divide and conquer: split the job

In plain words

Sorting a thousand cards is hard. Sorting two piles of five hundred is easier, and merging two sorted piles is easy. Split, solve the pieces the same way, then combine.

Binary search (part 4) is another example, with a twist: it splits the sorted list in half, throws one half away, and continues on the other.

Expert viewThe recurrence, in words

The cost of such an algorithm is described by a recurrence. Merge sort sorts two halves, each half the size, and then does work proportional to n to merge. In words: the time for n items equals twice the time for n over 2 items, plus about n. That solves to O(n log n), because there are about log n levels of halving and each level does about n work in total. Binary search does one half-sized piece plus a constant amount of work: the time for n equals the time for n over 2, plus a constant, which solves to O(log n). The master theorem is a general rule that solves many recurrences of this shape. Divide and conquer fits best when the pieces are independent, which also makes them easy to run in parallel. If pieces overlap heavily, dynamic programming is usually the better tool.

Backtracking: try, undo, try again

The classic example is the N-queens puzzle: place N chess queens on an N by N board so that none attacks another (no two share a row, column or diagonal). Take the 4 by 4 board and place one queen per row, going down.

In plain words

The trick is that you give up on a bad start immediately. As soon as row 3 has no safe square, you do not bother with row 4. That saves a great deal of work.

Expert viewPruning and cost

Backtracking explores a tree of partial solutions, and pruning cuts a branch as soon as a constraint is broken. In the worst case the search can still be exponential, because pruning only helps as much as the constraints let it; the 4 by 4 board has just two solutions, but the search effort grows very quickly with N. Good practice is to check constraints as early as possible, choose the most constrained variable next (the idea behind many sudoku solvers), and keep fast data structures for "is this square attacked?" checks. Sudoku is the same pattern: fill a cell with a digit not already in its row, column or box; if a later cell has no legal digit, back up. Backtracking suits constraint puzzles, generating combinations and permutations, and finding any or all solutions.

Choosing between them

StyleIdeaGood whenWatch out for
GreedyTake the best-looking choice, never undoA proven greedy property holdsCan be wrong without a proof
Divide and conquerSplit, solve pieces, combinePieces are independent and combining is cheapOverlapping pieces repeat work
Dynamic programmingSplit, store overlapping answersOverlapping subproblems and optimal substructureTable memory
BacktrackingTry, undo, try againConstraint puzzles and "find all solutions"Possible exponential time

Try it by hand

Greedy never looks back, divide and conquer shares the load, backtracking is allowed to say "oops".

Glossary
Greedy algorithm
Builds a solution by always taking the choice that looks best at that moment.
Divide and conquer
Split a problem into smaller copies, solve them, combine the results.
Backtracking
Build a solution step by step and undo steps that lead to dead ends.
Pruning
Cutting off a search branch as soon as it cannot lead to a solution.
Recurrence
A formula for the cost of a problem in terms of the cost of smaller ones.
Constraint
A rule a solution must obey, such as no two queens attack.
Exchange argument
A proof that swapping in the greedy choice does not hurt.
Optimal
The best possible answer according to the goal.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know whether greedy works?

You need an argument or proof for the problem. A good habit is to compare greedy with an exhaustive method on small cases and look for a counter-example.

Is backtracking just brute force?

It is a smarter search: it abandons partial solutions early. In the worst case it can still be very slow.

Is merge sort the only divide and conquer algorithm?

No. Binary search, quick sort and fast Fourier transforms are other well-known examples.

Can I mix these styles?

Yes. Many practical solutions combine them, for example backtracking with greedy ordering of choices to find solutions sooner.

Key takeaways

  • Greedy is fast but only correct when local choices add up to the best overall
  • Divide and conquer splits a problem, solves the pieces and combines them
  • Backtracking tries choices and undoes them, pruning dead ends early
