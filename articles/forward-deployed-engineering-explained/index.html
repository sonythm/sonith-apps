Forward Deployed Engineering explained

What a forward deployed engineer does, where the term comes from, how the role differs from a software engineer, solutions engineer, consultant or product manager, and how AI is making it more important.

Most software is built far away from the people who use it. A forward deployed engineer (often shortened to FDE) does the opposite: they go to where the problem is, work beside the people who have it, and build or adapt software until it works in the real world. The role has been getting a lot of attention, so this article explains it plainly.

The short version

A forward deployed engineer is a software engineer embedded with customers. They learn how a customer's business really runs, then make technology work inside it, and they stay close enough to see whether it helped.

The chef analogy

Imagine two ways to get a good meal. A restaurant has a fixed menu and a kitchen you never see. A chef who comes to your home looks in your fridge, asks who is eating and what they like, and cooks something that suits you, using your pots. The restaurant is efficient. The visiting chef fits. A forward deployed engineer is the visiting chef of software.

In plain words

The product team builds the kitchen and the recipes. The forward deployed engineer takes them into your kitchen and makes the meal work for your table.

Where the term comes from

The practice is most closely associated with Palantir, a US software company, which has used the title Forward Deployed Software Engineer for engineers who deploy its platforms at customers, and nicknames the role "Delta" inside the company. Embedding engineers with customers is older than any single company, for example vendors who sent engineers to customer sites to install and customise systems, but the FDE label spread widely after Palantir's use of it. I have not been able to confirm exactly when the public term first appeared, so treat any single origin story with care. This article has no connection with Palantir.

What the work looks like

  1. Discover. Sit with the users. Watch the real process. Ask why, and ask what happens when things go wrong.
  2. Prototype. Build something small that works well enough to try, fast.
  3. Deploy. Put it into real use, in the customer's environment, with their data and constraints.
  4. Feedback. Watch what happens. Fix what is wrong. Pass what you learn back to the product team.

Then repeat. The loop is short on purpose. The shorter the distance between a real problem and a working solution, the faster everyone learns.

Real example

A warehouse team re-enters the same details in two systems every day. A forward deployed engineer spends a day on the floor, sees where the double entry happens, builds a small connector, tries it with one shift, and adjusts it after the team points out an exception nobody had mentioned.

How it differs from nearby roles

RoleWhat they mainly doTime with customers
Software engineerBuilds the product for many customers, usually from a distance.Little with any one customer
Forward deployed engineerBuilds and adapts software for specific customers, on site or close by.A lot
Solutions engineerOften supports sales: demos the product, designs how it would fit, helps with early set-up.A lot, mostly before and during set-up
ConsultantAdvises on strategy or process, and may not build the software.A lot
Product managerDecides what the product should do, across all customers.Some

These are simplified. Titles vary between companies, and some people do several of these jobs.

Skills that matter

  • Strong engineering basics. You must build things that work.
  • Fast learning. Every customer has a new domain, new tools and new vocabulary.
  • Listening. The real problem is often not the one first described.
  • Communication. Explaining technical choices to non-technical people.
  • Ownership. You stay with a problem until it works.
  • Judgement. Knowing when a quick fix is enough and when something must be built properly.

Why AI makes the role more important

Anthropic's announcement of its Claude Frontier Academy on 2 October 2026 gives one view. It says the main obstacle to enterprise AI is talent, meaning the people who can make AI work inside a real business, whom it calls the "hardest talent to find". It committed $100 million to train 10,000 Frontier Deployed Engineers by the end of 2027, in a programme where engineers lead a real use case inside their own organisation. In its words, these engineers can "take Claude from an idea to a system in production".

The idea is simple: powerful AI models are widely available, but turning one into something that reliably helps a particular business takes people who understand both the technology and the work. That is what forward deployed engineers do. AI tools also make the prototype step faster, which makes the loop tighter.

How to become one

  1. Get good at building. Learn one stack well, and ship small real projects.
  2. Practise discovery. Build something for a real person, watch them use it, and change it.
  3. Learn a domain. Warehousing, healthcare, finance, factories. Depth in an area makes you valuable.
  4. Use AI tools well. Learn their strengths and limits, and always review the output.
  5. Work close to users. Roles in support engineering, implementation or solutions are common routes in.
  6. Show your work. A portfolio of problems solved matters more than a title.

The core of forward deployed engineering is a habit: stay close to the problem, build something real, and bring what you learn back.

Glossary
Forward deployed
Working at or close to the customer's site rather than in the company's office.
Embedded
Sitting within the customer's team.
Prototype
A quick early version built to test an idea.
Deployment
Putting software into real use.
Domain
The field a customer works in, such as logistics or banking.
Stakeholder
Anyone affected by or interested in the solution.

Frequently asked questions

Is forward deployed engineering a sales job?

No. It is an engineering job done close to customers. It can support sales, but the work is building and fixing.

Do FDEs travel a lot?

It depends on the company and the customers. Some work on site, some remotely with frequent contact.

Do I need a computer science degree?

Many FDEs have one, but what matters most is that you can build, learn fast and communicate. Check the requirements of any role you are interested in.

Is the role only for big technology companies?

No. Any organisation that rolls out complex software to customers may use some version of it.
