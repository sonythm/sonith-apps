Software in finance, explained for non-technical people

Why software that handles money is built differently: ledgers and double-entry, accuracy, audit trails, reconciliation, idempotency, security and a compliance mindset. General education only.

Reading level

When a photo app has a bug, a picture looks odd. When software that handles money has a bug, someone is out of pocket and trust is damaged. That single fact shapes how financial software is designed. This article explains the main ideas in plain language, picture first, with an Expert view in each section. It is general education only, not financial or legal advice, and it does not describe any specific organisation's systems.

The ledger: the oldest idea in the book

A ledger is a record of money movements. For hundreds of years, businesses have kept ledgers: a list of entries saying what came in, what went out and when.

In plain words

Financial software is, at heart, a very careful ledger. Balances, statements and reports are all calculated from the entries, never typed in by hand.

Double-entry: every entry has two sides

In double-entry bookkeeping, every transaction is recorded in at least two places. If 100 moves from account A to account B, account A goes down by 100 and account B goes up by 100. The two sides must always balance.

Money does not appear or disappear. It only moves.

Expert viewDebits, credits and the accounting equation

In formal accounting each entry has debits and credits that must total the same amount, and accounts are grouped (assets, liabilities, equity, income, expenses) so that the accounting equation, assets equal liabilities plus equity, always holds. A trial balance sums all debits and credits to confirm they match. Software that stores single-sided balances can drift silently; software that stores balanced entries can detect an error the moment the totals disagree.

Never erase, only add

A bank statement does not rub out last week's mistake. If an entry was wrong, a new correcting entry is added that reverses it, and the history stays intact.

Expert viewAppend-only and audit trails

In software terms a ledger is append-only: records are inserted but never updated or deleted. Corrections are new records that reference the original. This makes an audit trail possible: a trustworthy history of what happened, when and who did it. Practical designs add immutable timestamps, user identifiers and reason fields, and prevent changes at the database or permission level rather than relying on developers' discipline.

Accuracy: why computers are careful with money

Computers can make tiny rounding mistakes when they store decimal numbers in certain ways, and a tiny mistake repeated millions of times becomes real money.

Text
Risky:   price = 19.99, quantity = 3  ->  total from decimals, may drift
Safer:   price = 1999 (in smallest unit), quantity = 3  ->  total = 5997 exactly
Expert viewRounding rules

Careful systems store amounts as integers of the smallest unit, or in exact decimal types, and write down the rounding rule (for example round half to even, or always round down for fees) and apply it identically everywhere. Percentage splits need an explicit rule for the leftover unit so that parts always sum to the whole. Currency conversion adds the question of which rate and which time, and the rate used should be stored with the transaction.

Idempotency: pressing "pay" twice

Imagine you tap "pay", nothing seems to happen, and you tap again. Did you pay twice? Good financial software is designed so that sending the same request twice has the same effect as sending it once. This property is called idempotency.

In plain words

Each payment request carries a unique reference. If the system sees the same reference again, it returns the original result instead of moving money a second time.

Expert viewDesigning for repeats and failures

Networks time out, clients retry, and message queues deliver "at least once", so duplicates are normal. The standard defence is an idempotency key stored with the result, checked inside the same transaction that applies the change. Related ideas: retry with backoff, treat unknown outcomes as "pending" and reconcile later, and keep operations small and atomic. A database transaction groups the steps (debit, credit, record) so that either all happen or none do.

Reconciliation: checking against the other side

A company's records and the bank's records should agree. Reconciliation compares them entry by entry and investigates differences.

Real example

At the end of the day a shop's till says it took 50 payments. The payment provider's report says 49 were settled. Reconciliation finds the missing one, still pending. The next day it arrives and the books match.

Expert viewMatching rules

Matching typically uses amount, date window and reference, with tolerance for timing differences. Unmatched items are classified (timing, fee, duplicate, missing, wrong amount) and aged: an item unresolved for days is escalated. Mature teams track the reconciliation break rate and its causes, because a rising rate is an early warning of a systemic bug.

Security and the compliance mindset

Money attracts attackers, so financial software is built defensively, and it is regulated.

Careful

This article is general education. Rules differ by country and by type of business, so real systems need qualified legal and compliance advice.

Expert viewBuilding for the auditor

Engineers in this field learn to ask early, "who might audit this, and what will they ask?" Design for explainability: every figure traceable to entries, every change attributable, retention periods respected, sensitive items (card numbers, personal documents) minimised or tokenised, and access reviewed regularly. It is a mindset more than a checklist: be exact, be traceable, assume you will be asked to prove it.

Glossary
Ledger
The record of all money movements, from which balances are calculated.
Double-entry
Recording each transaction as two matching entries.
Append-only
Records can be added but never edited or deleted.
Audit trail
A complete, trustworthy history of what happened and who did it.
Idempotency
Doing the same request twice has the same result as doing it once.
Reconciliation
Comparing two sets of records to find and explain differences.
Database transaction
A group of steps that all succeed or all fail together.
Settlement
The final transfer that completes a payment between parties.

Frequently asked questions

Is this financial advice?

No. It is general education about how financial software is designed.

Why not just use ordinary decimal numbers?

Because small rounding differences add up. Exact whole-number amounts and clear rounding rules avoid that.

What is the biggest risk in money software?

Silent errors, such as a double charge or a missing entry, that nobody notices until a customer does. Design aims to make errors loud, not silent.

  • Records are added, never erased
