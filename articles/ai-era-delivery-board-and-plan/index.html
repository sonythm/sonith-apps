Planning short delivery cycles in the AI era: the board and the plan

A practical playbook for planning one-to-three day delivery cycles when AI does much of the building: the board, a 30 to 45 minute planning ritual, sizing by verification effort, and a worked week.

  • If you cannot say how you will verify it, it is not ready
  • Borrow proven foundations and label your own adaptations

Reading level

This is Part A of a two-part playbook for a solo developer or a team of two to five who use AI to do much of the building and want to deliver in short cycles, from one day up to a week. Part A covers the board and the plan. Part B covers roles, meetings, quality gates and release. It follows on from Agile in the age of AI. If you are new to the idea of describing software and accepting what the AI writes, read Vibe coding explained first.

Careful

Believable, doable, honest. Everything here can be done next week with a free tool or a paper board. Each section carries one of two labels. Proven foundation means a long-established, widely used practice, sourced from the official Scrum Guide, the Kanban Guide or the Agile Manifesto. My adaptation means my own way of applying those ideas to AI-assisted work. It is not an industry standard, and I have no independent evidence that it works for everyone. Try it small and keep what helps.

The whole system in one picture

My adaptation (not an industry standard)

The combination is my adaptation: a small-batch delivery loop with a human who decides and verifies, and an AI that builds. The parts it borrows are classic: ordered backlog, small batches, work-in-progress limits, a goal for each cycle, and a retrospective.

The board for short cycles

Proven foundation

A board that makes the workflow visible, and a limit on work in progress, are long-standing Kanban ideas. The Kanban Guide defines work in progress as "the number of work items started but not finished", and names three practices: defining and visualizing a workflow, actively managing items in it, and improving the workflow. A goal for each cycle comes from Scrum, where the Sprint Goal is "the single objective for the Sprint". Checked at scrumguides.org and kanbanguides.org on 2026-10-21.

My adaptation (not an industry standard)

The AI-era columns (Briefed, AI building, Verify, Ship to testers, Learn), the "verification debt" counter and the risk and review-size tags on cards are my adaptation. They exist because with AI the queue builds up at verification, and I want that queue visible.

Expert viewWhy these columns and these limits

The columns follow the path of one change: an idea becomes a brief, the AI builds from the brief, a person verifies, a small test group sees it, and what was learned is recorded. A limit on "AI building" of one item keeps the AI from producing a pile of changes you cannot review; a limit on "Verify" of two forces you to review before asking for more. A paper board works: use sticky notes and write the limit on the column header. The verification debt counter is simply a count of cards in Verify plus any built but unreviewed; when it reaches the limit, you stop starting new work and review. That rule, finish before starting, is the heart of limiting work in progress.

The planning ritual: 30 to 45 minutes

Proven foundation

Scrum describes sprint planning around three questions: why is this sprint valuable, what can be done, and how will the chosen work get done. The Developers plan the work, and the sprint goal is the single objective. The same three questions fit a one-day cycle. The Scrum Guide gives time-boxes as maximums (up to eight hours for a one-month sprint, shorter for shorter sprints), not targets.

My adaptation (not an industry standard)

Writing a brief and a verify plan for each slice, deciding what the AI does and what a human decides, and agreeing a stop or rollback condition are my additions for AI-assisted work. The 30 to 45 minute length is what has worked for small cycles for me, not a rule.

Expert viewRunning the ritual quickly

Start from the outcome, not the list of tasks. Slice vertically (a thin piece through every layer that a user can notice), then ask of each slice: how will I know it works, how big a diff will it produce, and what is the worst that could go wrong. Write the brief (see Part B for a template), decide the ship target (a small test group, not everyone), and block calendar time for review before any building starts. Stop when the plan is credible; leave the detailed task list for the first slice only, because the plan changes as you learn.

Sizing work for AI: by verification, not typing

In plain words

Typing used to be the expensive part, so we sized work by how long it took to write. When an AI types, the expensive parts are deciding what is wanted and checking that it is right. So size by how hard a change is to verify, and how bad it would be if it were wrong.

If you cannot say how you will verify it, it is not ready.

My adaptation (not an industry standard)

Sizing by verification effort and risk is my adaptation. It builds on the long-standing advice to keep batches small and to slice work vertically, and on teams' habit of sizing relatively with a simple scale. Whatever scale you use, keep it rough and agree it together.

Capacity: your attention is the limit

PersonFocused hours per day (illustrative)Share for review and checkingHours for briefs and decisions
Solo developerabout 4about 40%about 1
Two-person team, eachabout 4about 40%about 1
Part-time helperabout 2about 40%about 0.5

Capacity here is the number of hours of real attention, not lines of code. A solo developer with four focused hours a day has about twenty hours in a five-day week, of which around eight go on review and verification and around five on briefs and decisions. The numbers are illustrative; yours will differ, so measure for a couple of weeks and adjust.

Proven foundation

Planning from real capacity, after leave, meetings and unplanned work, is classic practice; the Scrum Guide says the Developers plan the sprint and adapt the plan daily towards the goal.

Expert viewWhy fewer things in flight finishes things sooner

A simple relationship from queueing theory, known as Little's Law, says that for a stable process the average time an item spends in the system equals the average number of items in progress divided by the average rate at which items finish. In plain words: if you finish about two changes a day and have ten in progress, each takes about five days; with four in progress, about two days. That is why limiting work in progress shortens cycle time without anyone working faster. It is a general result, not a prediction for your project, and it assumes steady flow.

How this relates to Scrum and Kanban

Proven foundation

Scrum and Kanban are both described in official guides (the Scrum Guide at scrumguides.org and the Kanban Guide at kanbanguides.org). This playbook is not Scrum or Kanban; it borrows their foundations and labels what is mine. The Agile Manifesto's values (individuals and interactions, working software, customer collaboration, responding to change) apply unchanged.

Key idea

Five steps you can do tomorrow with a free tool or a paper board:

Glossary
Cycle
One short loop of planning, building, verifying, shipping and learning.
Brief
A short written description of an outcome, checks and limits for one slice.
Slice
A thin, verifiable piece of work that goes through every layer.
WIP limit
A cap on how many items may be in progress at once.
Verification debt
Changes built but not yet reviewed or tested.
Sprint goal
In Scrum, the single objective for the sprint.
Retrospective
A short meeting to improve how the team works.

Frequently asked questions

Is this Scrum?

No. It borrows foundations from Scrum and Kanban and says clearly which parts are classic and which are my adaptation.

Can a solo developer do this?

Yes. You hold all the roles, so book time for the verifying hat explicitly. Part B has a hat checklist.

Do I need special software?

No. Paper and sticky notes work, as do free board tools.

Is there evidence that one-to-three day cycles are better?

Not that I can point to. It is my own practice, and the labels say so. Start with a one-week cycle if shorter feels too fast.

Key takeaways

  • Plan around your attention and review time, not typing speed
