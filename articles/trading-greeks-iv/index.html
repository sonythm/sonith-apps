The Greeks and implied volatility in plain words

Delta, gamma, theta, vega and rho are the dials that show how an option's price tends to react. Implied volatility is the market's uncertainty expressed as one number. Illustrative values only.

  • The Greeks are dials showing how an option price tends to react to one change at a time
  • Implied volatility is backed out of the option price, it is not a forecast
  • Every Greek is local and changes, so none is a fixed promise

Reading level

After the basics of options, people meet a row of Greek letters. They sound frightening but each answers a simple question: if one thing changes, roughly how much does the option price react? This part uses a car dashboard as the picture, then an Expert view gives the standard definitions. All numbers are illustrative.

The dashboard idea

In plain words

A car dashboard has a speedometer, a fuel gauge and a temperature dial. None tells you everything, but together they describe the state of the car. The Greeks are an option's dials. Each one isolates one influence and says how sensitive the price is to it, while the others are held still. Real markets never hold the others still, which is the first limit to keep in mind.

The next sections read each dial in turn.

Delta: how much the price tends to move

In plain words

Delta is like gear ratio. If delta is 0.50, a 1 unit rise in the underlying tends to lift the option price by about 0.50. A call has delta between 0 and 1; a put has delta between 0 and minus 1, because a put gains when the underlying falls.

For example, with a share priced at 100 and a call delta of 0.50 (illustrative), a move to 101 would lift the option price by roughly 0.50. A call far out of the money has a delta near 0; one deep in the money has a delta near 1.

Some people read delta as a rough chance that the option finishes in the money. Treat that with caution: it is a loose reading of the number, not a probability forecast.

Expert viewDelta, precisely

Delta is the first partial derivative of the option price with respect to the underlying price. It is also used to size the quantity of the underlying that offsets an option's price sensitivity, a practice called delta hedging, which needs frequent adjustment because delta moves. Delta is a local measure: it describes tiny moves around the current price, and for larger moves it becomes less accurate.

Gamma: how fast delta changes

In plain words

If delta is your speed, gamma is how hard your foot is on the pedal. A high gamma means the option's sensitivity can swing quickly, so its price can change by more than a fixed delta suggests.

Expert viewGamma, precisely

Gamma is the second partial derivative of the option price with respect to the underlying price, or the rate of change of delta. It is usually highest for options near the strike with little time left. A buyer of an option has positive gamma; a seller has negative gamma, which means the seller's exposure can change unfavourably when the underlying moves sharply.

Theta: the clock

In plain words

Theta is the melting ice cube. An option's time value shrinks as expiry nears, even if nothing else changes. A theta of minus 0.05 (illustrative) means about 0.05 is lost from the price per day, all else equal. It usually works against the buyer and in favour of the seller.

Expert viewTheta, precisely

Theta is the partial derivative of the option price with respect to time. Quoted per day or per year depending on the platform, so check the unit. It is not constant: for options near the strike it tends to grow in size close to expiry. For a long option it is usually negative, because time value fades.

Vega and implied volatility

First, volatility. It is a number describing how widely a price swings. Historical volatility looks backward: how much did the price actually move over a past window? Implied volatility looks at today's option price and asks: what level of volatility would the pricing model need to produce this price? It is backed out of the price, the way you can work out the interest rate from a loan repayment.

In plain words

Think of a weather forecast of "stormy". If a storm warning is issued, umbrella prices rise even before a drop of rain falls. In the same way, when people become more uncertain, options get dearer even if the underlying price has not moved. That is vega at work.

Implied volatility is not a forecast and not a promise. It reflects what the market is willing to pay for protection or exposure today, and it can differ across strikes and expiry dates.

Expert viewVega, rho and the model behind them

Vega is the partial derivative of the option price with respect to volatility (strictly not a Greek letter, but conventionally grouped with them). It is generally larger for options with more time left. Rho is the partial derivative with respect to the interest rate; it is usually the smallest effect for short-dated options and is mentioned for completeness.

These definitions come from the classic family of option pricing models. Such a model takes inputs (underlying price, strike, time, rate, volatility) and outputs a price, resting on assumptions: smooth price paths, constant volatility, no costs, no jumps. Real markets break these assumptions, so implied volatility is best read as a quoting convention that converts a price into a comparable number, not as a measurement of truth.

Reading the dials together

In plain words

No single dial tells the story. Picture an option with the illustrative readings above: delta 0.50, gamma 0.04, theta minus 0.05, vega 0.12. If the share moves up 1 unit, delta says the price tends to rise by about 0.50, and gamma says delta itself would creep up to about 0.54. If a day passes with nothing else changing, theta says about 0.05 fades away. If implied volatility rises by 1 point, vega says about 0.12 is added. In real life all three happen at once and the effects mix, sometimes cancelling out and sometimes adding up.

The limits of the dials

Careful

Greeks describe how a price tends to respond, not what will happen. Options can lose their entire premium, and sellers can face losses far beyond the premium received. A dashboard reading is no substitute for understanding the risk.

The Greeks are dials, not forecasts: each one shows a sensitivity at this moment.

Frequently asked questions

Why does an option get dearer when the price has not moved?

Because implied volatility can rise, which lifts time value. Vega measures this.

Is implied volatility a prediction?

No. It is what the market's pricing implies today, and it may turn out different from what actually happens.

Do I need to know all the Greeks?

This article is education only. It explains what the dials mean, nothing more.

Are the numbers real?

No. All values are illustrative.

Careful

Educational content, not investment advice. Trading, especially with leverage and options, can lose more than you expect.
