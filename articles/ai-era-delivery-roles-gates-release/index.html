The AI-era delivery system: roles, meetings, quality gates and release

Reading level

This is Part B of the playbook. Part A covers the board and the plan; this part covers the people and the safety: roles, meetings, ready and done, quality gates, release and rollback, and sharing context. It is written for a solo developer or a team of two to five.

Careful

Labels used here. Proven foundation marks long-established, widely used practices from the Scrum Guide, the Kanban Guide and the Agile Manifesto (checked on 2026-10-21 at scrumguides.org, kanbanguides.org and agilemanifesto.org). My adaptation marks my own way of applying them to AI-assisted work. It is not an industry standard and I do not claim independent evidence for it. Everything is doable next week with a free tool or paper.

Roles: who briefs, builds, verifies and ships

Proven foundation

The Scrum Guide describes three accountabilities: the Product Owner (one person who orders the backlog and maximises the value of the product), the Developers (who plan the work and build to the Definition of Done) and the Scrum Master (who helps everyone use Scrum well). A Scrum Team is typically ten or fewer people. The idea that one person owns the ordering of the work, and that the people doing the work own the plan, carries over to any small team.

My adaptation (not an industry standard)

Splitting the work into briefer, builder, verifier and shipper is my adaptation for AI-assisted delivery. Two rules I use: the person who writes the brief should not be the only person who checks the result when a team is available, and an AI never owns verification or release.

The hat checklist (for a solo developer)

Expert viewWhy one hat at a time

People find their own mistakes badly, and judging a change straight after prompting for it invites accepting it. Changing hat (even taking a short break, or reviewing the next morning) is a cheap way to simulate a second pair of eyes. In a team, rotate the verifier role so the same person is not always the bottleneck, and avoid verifying your own large changes.

Meetings: three short ones

Proven foundation

The Scrum Guide's Daily Scrum is a 15-minute event for the Developers to inspect progress towards the Sprint Goal and adapt the plan; the Sprint Review inspects the outcome with stakeholders; the Sprint Retrospective plans ways to improve quality and effectiveness. Their time-boxes are maximums for a one-month sprint, and shrink for shorter ones.

My adaptation (not an industry standard)

Shortening them to ten, fifteen and fifteen minutes, and making the retro end with a written change to the instruction file, is my adaptation.

Ready and done

Proven foundation

The Definition of Done is part of the Scrum Guide: "a formal description of the state of the Increment when it meets the quality measures required for the product". A Definition of Ready is a common team practice, not part of the Scrum Guide.

Backlog refinement: writing AI-ready briefs

Refinement is where the backlog becomes ready to build. In the Scrum Guide it is an ongoing activity that adds detail, order and size to items, not one of the five events.

My adaptation (not an industry standard)

Treating the instruction file and skills library as assets that grow with every retro is my adaptation. For sharing them with a team, see Reuse context and share it with your team.

Estimating

Keep it rough. Size slices by verification effort and risk (Part A), use a simple scale such as small, medium and large, and treat anything large or high risk as a signal to split. The Scrum Guide says the Developers who will do the work are responsible for the sizing and does not mandate a scale. Do not turn sizes into promised dates.

Quality gates

Proven foundation

Automated tests, code review and small, frequent releases are long-established engineering practice. Treat generated code like any other code: it needs the same checks, and a little more suspicion.

Expert viewMaking the gates real

Write the gates into the Definition of Done so that skipping one is a visible decision. For safety checks, keep a short list tuned to your product (for example: no secrets in the code, no new data leaving the device, input validation on new fields). Be wary of an AI that makes failing tests pass by weakening them, and read the tests as well as the green result. When a gate fails twice for the same reason, add a line to the instruction file so it fails earlier next time.

Release and rollback

Proven foundation

Releasing in small steps, with a way to go back, is long-standing practice (feature switches and staged releases are widely used terms). The rollback condition itself, the specific signal at which you undo a release, should be written before you ship.

Expert viewChoosing the rollback signal

Decide in advance what you will watch and what counts as bad. Typical signals for a small app are crashes or errors reported by the test group, a key action that stops working (for example saving a record), or reports of data looking wrong. Write the rule as a sentence ("if two testers report lost data, switch the feature off") so that the decision is not made under pressure. After any rollback, write down what happened and what check would have caught it, and add that check to the Done list.

Sharing context in a team

Commit the instruction file, the skills folder and the brief templates, review changes to them like code, and keep secrets out. The team-sharing article covers where each file lives and how to share them.

Careful

Never put passwords, keys or personal data in instruction files, skills, briefs or logs, even in a private repository.

Small cycles are safe only when someone checks, someone can undo, and everyone learns.

Glossary
Hat
One of the roles a solo developer plays in turn: briefer, builder, verifier, shipper.
Definition of Done
The shared description of what finished means.
Definition of Ready
A checklist an item meets before work starts.
Quality gate
A check a change must pass before moving on.
Staged release
Releasing to a small group first, then widening.
Rollback
Undoing a release or switching a feature off.
Instruction file
A project file of standing rules that an AI assistant reads each session.
Refinement
Ongoing work that makes upcoming items clear, ordered and sized.

Frequently asked questions

Do we need all four roles on a small team?

You need all four jobs done. People can combine them, as long as large changes are verified by someone other than the author when possible.

Why not let the AI review its own work?

It can help find problems, but the responsibility to decide that something is correct and safe stays with a person.

How often should we release?

As often as you can verify safely. Small and frequent usually beats large and rare.

Is this an official method?

No. It builds on official guides and labels my own adaptations clearly.
