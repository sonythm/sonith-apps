What is Claude AI? A plain-language guide

Who makes Claude, what a language model is, what it can and cannot do, and how people use it, with the newest models as of 21 October 2026.

If you have heard people say "ask Claude" and wondered what they mean, this is for you. No technical background is needed. By the end you will know what Claude is, who makes it, what it is good at, where it goes wrong, and how to try it safely.

The short answer

Claude is an AI assistant. You type (or paste, or upload) something, and it writes back. You can ask it to explain an idea, draft an email, summarise a long document, look at a picture, or help write computer code. It is made by a company called Anthropic.

In plain words

Think of Claude as a very well-read assistant who works at the speed of typing. It has read an enormous amount of text, it can write fluently about almost anything, and it can still make mistakes. You are the manager. It does the first draft, you check it.

Who makes it

Claude is made by Anthropic, a company that describes itself as a public benefit corporation. Its stated mission is "AI research and products that put safety at the frontier". That does not mean Claude is perfectly safe. It means safety is part of what the company says it is trying to do, and later in this article we look at what that looks like in practice.

This article is an independent explanation. I have no connection with Anthropic.

What is a language model?

Claude belongs to a kind of software called a large language model. The idea is easier than the name.

Imagine your phone suggesting the next word as you type a message. Now imagine that suggestion engine trained on a huge library of books, articles, websites and computer code, and made far more capable. Given some text, it works out what text should come next, then the next piece, and so on. A reply is built one small piece at a time.

That simple loop turns out to be powerful. To continue text well, a model has to pick up grammar, facts, styles, and patterns of reasoning. That is why one tool can help with a poem, a spreadsheet formula and a legal-sounding letter.

Key idea

A language model does not look things up like a search engine, and it does not "know" things the way you do. It produces the most fitting text it can. Usually that is accurate. Sometimes it is confidently wrong.

The Claude family, as of 21 October 2026

Anthropic does not make one Claude. It makes a family of models of different sizes, a bit like a range of cars. Anthropic's documentation lists four current models, and says all of them accept text and images and can use tools:

  • Claude Fable 5.1 is for demanding reasoning and long, multi-step agentic work. It is the slowest of the four.
  • Claude Opus 5.5 is for long-running coding and knowledge work. Anthropic suggests starting here for most workloads.
  • Claude Sonnet 5.5 is described as the best combination of speed and intelligence.
  • Claude Haiku 5.5 is the fastest, meant for high-volume jobs such as sorting and extracting information.

According to Anthropic's news page, Fable 5.1 was announced on 15 September 2026, Opus 5.5 on 22 September, Sonnet 5.5 on 28 September and Haiku 5.5 on 7 October. Anthropic also mentions Claude Mythos models with limited availability. I have not covered those here. Model lineups change often, so treat this list as a snapshot and check the official page linked at the end.

What Claude can do

  • Explain and teach. Ask for an explanation of anything at the level you choose: "explain this to a ten-year-old".
  • Write and edit. Emails, reports, summaries, rewrites in a different tone.
  • Read long material. The current models can hold a very large amount of text in view at once. Anthropic's documentation says the 1 million token window is roughly 555,000 words.
  • Look at images. Photos of documents, charts or screenshots can be described and discussed.
  • Help with code. Writing, explaining and fixing software is one of its best-known uses.
  • Work with tools. Claude can be connected to other software, so it can search, run calculations or act on files.
Real example

A small business owner pastes in a long supplier contract and asks: "List the dates and amounts I must pay, in a table, and flag anything unusual." Claude produces the table in seconds. The owner still reads the flagged clauses themselves before signing anything.

What Claude cannot do (or does badly)

  • It can be wrong. Models sometimes state invented facts, names or numbers with complete confidence. People call this "hallucination".
  • Its memory is limited. What it knows about your conversation is mostly what is on the page in front of it. Some Claude products offer a memory feature, so check what yours does.
  • It is not a professional. It can help you prepare for a conversation with a doctor, lawyer or accountant. It does not replace one.
  • It cannot see the future. Anthropic lists a reliable knowledge cutoff of June 2026 for the current models. Anything after that, it only knows if it is given the information or can search for it.
Careful

For anything important, such as money, health, legal or safety decisions, treat Claude's answer as a draft to check against a trusted source.

Claude Code and agents

Beyond chat, there is Claude Code, which Anthropic describes as an agentic coding tool that reads your codebase, edits files, runs commands and works with development tools. It runs in a terminal, in code editors, as a desktop app and in a web browser.

"Agent" is the important word. A chat answers once. An agent is given a goal, then takes steps: it looks around, makes a plan, tries something, checks the result, and continues. Anthropic's documentation says Claude Code can even run several agents in parallel, with one coordinating the others. The later parts of this series cover this properly.

How Anthropic describes its approach to safety

Anthropic's research pages name teams working on alignment (helping models be helpful, honest and harmless), interpretability (studying how models work inside) and a Frontier Red Team that stress-tests systems. In its announcement for Opus 5.5, the company says external evaluators tested the model before release, that it is more resistant to prompt injection than its predecessor, and that it is less likely to take hard-to-reverse actions.

It is also candid about limits. The same announcement says the model often suspects it is being evaluated, which limits how well testing predicts real-world behaviour. That honesty is worth noticing. Safety here is ongoing work, not a finished fact.

Privacy basics

Anything you type is information you are handing to a service. A few sensible habits:

  • Do not paste passwords, bank details or other people's private data.
  • Look at the privacy and data settings of the product you use, because they can change and differ between personal and business plans.
  • For work, use the version your employer has approved.

How to start

  1. Open claude.ai and try a small, low-stakes task, such as summarising an article you already know.
  2. Be specific about what you want, and say who the answer is for.
  3. Check the result against what you know. Ask follow-up questions when something looks off.
  4. Move on to the next parts of this series for habits that make answers better.
Glossary
Language model
Software trained on large amounts of text to continue text sensibly.
Prompt
What you type to Claude, including any files or instructions.
Hallucination
A confident-sounding answer that is false or invented.
Agent
An AI that takes several steps toward a goal, using tools, instead of answering once.
Context window
How much text the model can hold in view at one time.

Frequently asked questions

Is Claude the same as ChatGPT?

No. They are different products from different companies, though both are language models and feel similar to use.

Is it free?

Anthropic offers a free plan and paid plans. Part 4 of this series explains plans and pricing, with the official page linked.

Does Claude think?

It produces useful reasoning-like text, but whether that is "thinking" is a debate. For practical purposes, treat it as a capable tool, not a person.

Can I trust it?

Trust it as you would a quick, clever colleague: useful, but verify what matters.
