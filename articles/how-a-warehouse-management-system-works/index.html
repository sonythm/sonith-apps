How a warehouse management system works

From the truck arriving to the parcel leaving, a picture-led tour of receiving, putaway, picking, packing and shipping, and the software that keeps track of every item.

Reading level

A warehouse looks simple from outside: goods come in, goods go out. Inside, thousands of items sit on shelves, orders arrive all day, and every item must be found quickly and counted correctly. The software that keeps this under control is a warehouse management system, or WMS. This article is general education and does not describe any particular company's system. Each section gives the plain picture first and an Expert view for people who want the mechanics.

What a WMS is for

A WMS answers three questions, all day long.

It tells workers where to put things, where to pick them from, and in what order, and it records every move so the numbers stay true.

The journey of goods

Expert viewWhat the records look like

Underneath, a WMS is a ledger of stock movements: each receipt, move, pick and adjustment is a record with who, what, from where, to where, when and why. On-hand quantity by location is the sum of those movements, not a number someone overwrites. Receiving often distinguishes expected quantity (from an advance shipping notice or purchase order) from received quantity, and tracks status such as quarantined or damaged so unavailable stock is not offered for picking.

Receiving: counting what really arrived

The paperwork says what should have come. The WMS records what did come. Differences are noted straight away, because sorting out a shortage at the dock is far easier than three weeks later.

Putaway: a place for everything

In plain words

Every shelf has an address, like a house. The system keeps the address book up to date.

Smart rules help: fast-selling items near the packing area, heavy items low, items that must stay cool in cold rooms.

Expert viewSlotting and location logic

Warehouses use a location hierarchy such as zone, aisle, rack, level and bin. Slotting is the practice of choosing where each item lives. Common rules include ABC classification (the fast movers placed closest to packing), weight and size limits per location, hazard and temperature segregation, and keeping items that are often ordered together near each other. Poor slotting shows up as long walking distances and congestion in popular aisles.

Picking: the busiest part

Picking is usually the most time-consuming job in a warehouse, so a lot of design goes into it.

The WMS chooses the method and the route. A good route avoids backtracking and saves hours of walking.

Expert viewTrade-offs between methods

Batch picking reduces walking but adds a sorting step and the risk of mixing items; zone picking suits large sites but needs hand-over points; wave picking smooths the workload and matches carrier cut-offs but can create idle time between waves. Expert planners choose per product mix and order profile (many small orders versus a few large ones), and measure picks per hour, travel distance and error rate to compare.

Packing and shipping

At packing, scanning each item against the order catches mistakes before the parcel leaves. The system chooses a box size, prints the label and records weight. Shipping groups parcels by carrier and destination and confirms dispatch. Tracking information then flows back to the customer.

Barcodes and scanners: how the system knows

Workers carry handheld devices, often called RF scanners because they talk to the system over radio and wifi. Every scan is a small record: who, what, where, when.

If it was not scanned, the system does not know it happened.

Inventory accuracy

The system's count must match the shelf. Differences creep in through damage, theft, mis-picks or items put in the wrong place.

Expert viewAdjustments and audit trail

When a count differs, the WMS records an adjustment with a reason code rather than silently overwriting the quantity, so there is an audit trail. Mature operations track inventory accuracy as a metric, investigate repeat offenders (certain locations, products or shifts) and correct causes such as confusing labels, similar-looking items or rushed putaway. Counting during quiet periods and counting high-value or fast-moving items more often are standard practice.

WMS versus ERP

In plain words

The ERP sends orders and expected deliveries to the WMS. The WMS sends back what was received, picked and shipped.

Expert viewIntegration pitfalls

Integrations are a common source of trouble: orders arriving twice or late, units of measure that differ between systems, and status updates that arrive out of order. Robust designs make messages idempotent (repeating one is harmless), include unique references, and reconcile the two systems' stock regularly. Latency matters too: if the floor system waits on a slow call to the ERP, workers wait.

What can go wrong in the software

Building a WMS means being careful about these things: records must never be lost or duplicated, every change should be traceable, and screens must be fast enough for people on their feet.

Glossary
WMS
Warehouse management system, software that directs and records warehouse work.
ERP
Enterprise resource planning, the company-wide business system.
SKU
Stock keeping unit, a unique code for a product type.
Putaway
Moving received goods to their storage locations.
Picking
Collecting items from storage to fulfil orders.
RF scanner
A handheld barcode reader connected wirelessly to the system.
Cycle count
Counting a small part of the stock regularly instead of everything at once.
Bin location
The exact address of a storage position.
Slotting
Choosing where each item should be stored.

Frequently asked questions

Is a WMS only for huge warehouses?

No. Small operations use simpler versions, even spreadsheets. A dedicated system pays off as volume and complexity grow.

Do robots replace the WMS?

No. Robots follow instructions, and the WMS is what gives the instructions and keeps the records.

What is the hardest part of building one?

Matching software to how people really work on the floor, and keeping the records exactly right.
