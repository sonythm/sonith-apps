How to get really good with Claude: ten practical habits

Clear prompts, context, examples, planning, iteration and checking, with before-and-after prompts, plus how to give Claude standing instructions.

Getting good with Claude is less about secret phrases and more about habits. The advice here follows Anthropic's own prompting guidance, translated into plain language, with my own examples. The before-and-after prompts are illustrations I wrote, not quotes.

Habit 1: Be clear and direct

Anthropic's guidance suggests thinking of Claude as a brilliant but new employee who does not know your norms or your workflow. The more precisely you explain what you want, the better the result. Its "golden rule": show your prompt to a colleague with little context and ask them to follow it. If they would be confused, Claude will be too.

Real example

Before: "Write something about our new product."

After: "Write a 120-word announcement email for existing customers about our new invoice-reminder feature. Friendly, no jargon, one clear call to action: try it from the Billing page."

Habit 2: Say who it is for and why

Context improves answers. Anthropic's example: instead of "never use ellipses", say your reply will be read aloud by a text-to-speech engine that cannot pronounce them. Claude generalises from the reason. In everyday use: tell Claude the audience, the purpose and any constraint that matters.

Habit 3: Show examples

Examples are one of the most reliable ways to steer format, tone and structure. Anthropic recommends relevant, varied examples, and suggests three to five for best results. Wrap them clearly so Claude can tell example from instruction.

Rewrite customer complaints into a calm one-line summary.

<example>
Input: "I've been waiting THREE WEEKS and nobody replies!!!"
Output: Customer has waited three weeks without a reply.
</example>

Now do this one: ...

Habit 4: Structure long prompts

When a prompt mixes instructions, documents and examples, labelling each part helps. Anthropic recommends tags such as <instructions>, <context> and <input> for this. For long documents, its guidance says to put the long material first and your question at the end, which in its tests improved quality noticeably.

Habit 5: Ask for a plan before a big job

For anything with several steps, ask first: "Before writing anything, list the steps you would take and the questions you have." You can correct the plan in seconds, which is far cheaper than correcting a finished piece of work.

Habit 6: Work in rounds

Treat the first answer as a draft. Reply with specific feedback: "Shorter. Keep the second paragraph. Make the tone less formal." Short rounds beat one giant prompt.

Habit 7: Ask Claude to check itself, then check it yourself

Ask: "What assumptions did you make? What might be wrong here?" It often finds its own gaps. But the final check is yours. For facts, ask for sources and open them. For numbers, recalculate a few. For code, run the tests.

Careful

Never act on money, health, legal or safety advice from any AI without checking a trusted source or a professional.

Habit 8: Give it the right material, not all of it

Because quality can drop as the context grows, give Claude the relevant pages rather than a whole folder. If a conversation has become long and tangled, ask for a summary of decisions so far and start a fresh chat with that summary.

Habit 9: Use projects and files for repeated work

Paid Claude plans include Projects, according to Anthropic's pricing page, which let you keep related files and instructions together so you do not repeat yourself. The idea is the same as a standing briefing: write down once who you are, what good looks like, and the usual format.

Habit 10: Write standing instructions for coding work

In Claude Code, Anthropic describes a file called CLAUDE.md that sits in your project. Claude Code reads it at the start of every session. You use it to set coding standards, architecture decisions, preferred libraries and review checklists. Claude Code can also build up its own notes as it works. Part 6 covers this.

# CLAUDE.md (example)
- Use TypeScript. Keep functions small.
- Run the tests before saying a task is done.
- Never edit files under /generated.

Four common mistakes

  1. Vague requests. "Make it better" gives Claude nothing to aim at. Say what better means: shorter, warmer, more formal, simpler words.
  2. Assuming it knows your situation. It only has the page. Paste in the facts it needs, or say what you leave out on purpose.
  3. Accepting the first answer. Drafts exist to be improved. One round of feedback usually makes a visible difference.
  4. Skipping the check. A fluent answer is not a correct answer. Check what you will act on.

A weekly routine that works

  • Pick one real task from your week and do the first draft with Claude.
  • Keep your best prompts in a note, and improve them.
  • Review what Claude got wrong, and add a line to your standing instructions to prevent it next time.
Key idea

Mastery is a loop: ask clearly, look carefully, correct, and save what worked.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to learn "prompt engineering"?

Not as a special skill. Clear writing, good examples and careful checking cover most of it.

Should I be polite to Claude?

Tone does not hurt, but clarity matters far more than politeness.

Longer prompts or shorter?

As long as needed to be clear, and no longer. Remove material that does not help.
