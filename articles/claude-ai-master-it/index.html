How to get really good with Claude: ten practical habits

Clear prompts, context, examples, planning, iteration and checking, with before-and-after prompts, and how to give Claude standing instructions.

Reading level

Getting good with Claude is less about secret phrases and more about habits. The advice here follows Anthropic's own prompting guidance, translated into plain language, with my own illustrative examples. The before-and-after prompts are mine, not quotes. Every habit has a quick picture, then an Expert view with the details.

Habit 1: Be clear and direct

Anthropic's guidance suggests thinking of Claude as a brilliant but new employee who does not know your norms or workflow. The more precisely you explain what you want, the better the result.

Expert viewThe colleague test

Anthropic's "golden rule" for clarity: show your prompt to a colleague with minimal context and ask them to follow it. If they would be confused, Claude will be too. It also advises giving instructions as numbered steps when order or completeness matters, and being explicit if you want "above and beyond" behaviour instead of expecting Claude to infer it.

Habit 2: Say who it is for, and why

Context improves answers. Anthropic's example: instead of "never use ellipses", explain that the reply will be read aloud by a text-to-speech engine that cannot pronounce them. Claude generalises from the reason.

Habit 3: Show examples

Examples are one of the most reliable ways to steer format, tone and structure.

Text
Rewrite customer complaints into a calm one-line summary.

<example>
Input: "I've been waiting THREE WEEKS and nobody replies!!!"
Output: Customer has waited three weeks without a reply.
</example>

Now do this one: ...
Expert viewWhat good examples look like

Anthropic recommends examples that are relevant (mirroring your real use), diverse (covering edge cases so Claude does not latch onto an accidental pattern) and clearly marked, wrapped in tags so Claude can tell example from instruction. It suggests three to five examples for best results, and says you can even ask Claude to judge your examples for relevance and diversity or to generate more from your first set.

Habit 4: Structure long prompts

When a prompt mixes instructions, documents and examples, labelling each part helps.

Expert viewTags and placement

Anthropic recommends consistent, descriptive tags such as <instructions>, <context> and <input>, nested when content has a natural hierarchy. For long inputs of about 20k tokens or more, its guidance says to put the longform data near the top, above your query, instructions and examples, and reports that queries at the end can improve response quality by up to 30 percent in its tests, especially for complex multi-document inputs.

Habit 5: Ask for a plan before a big job

For anything with several steps, ask first: "Before writing anything, list the steps you would take and the questions you have." Correcting a plan takes seconds. Correcting a finished piece of work takes much longer.

Habit 6: Work in rounds

Treat the first answer as a draft. Reply with specific feedback: "Shorter. Keep the second paragraph. Make the tone less formal." Short rounds beat one giant prompt.

Habit 7: Ask Claude to check itself, then check it yourself

Ask: "What assumptions did you make? What might be wrong here?" It often finds its own gaps. The final check is yours.

Careful

Never act on money, health, legal or safety advice from any AI without checking a trusted source or a professional.

Expert viewGrounding and investigation

Anthropic's coding guidance includes a sample instruction that tells Claude to investigate before answering: read the relevant files first and never make claims about code it has not opened. The same principle works outside code: give Claude the source material, ask it to quote the passages it relied on, and make "not stated in the material" an allowed answer.

Habit 8: Give it the right material, not all of it

Because quality can drop as the context grows (part 6), give Claude the relevant pages rather than a whole folder. If a conversation has become long and tangled, ask for a summary of decisions so far and start a fresh chat with that summary.

Habit 9: Use Projects for repeated work

Anthropic's support pages describe Projects as self-contained workspaces with their own chat histories and a knowledge base: files you add are reused across every chat in the project, and project instructions apply to every chat. Anthropic's pricing page lists Projects among Pro features. Chats inside a project do not share context with each other unless the information is in the project's knowledge. Part 9 covers sharing with a team.

Habit 10: Write standing instructions for coding work

In Claude Code, a file called CLAUDE.md holds instructions that Claude reads at the start of every session. Part 7 explains where it lives and what to put in it.

Text
# CLAUDE.md (example)
- Use TypeScript. Keep functions small.
- Run the tests before saying a task is done.
- Never edit files under /generated.

Four common mistakes

  1. Vague requests. "Make it better" gives Claude nothing to aim at. Say what better means.
  2. Assuming it knows your situation. It only has the page.
  3. Accepting the first answer. One round of feedback usually makes a visible difference.
  4. Skipping the check. A fluent answer is not a correct answer.

A weekly routine that works

Mastery is a loop: ask clearly, look carefully, correct, and save what worked.

Glossary
Few-shot examples
A few sample inputs and outputs shown in the prompt to set the pattern.
Prompt template
A saved prompt with blanks you fill in each time.
Project
A workspace that keeps files and instructions together for repeated work.
CLAUDE.md
A project file Claude Code reads at the start of each session for standing instructions.
Iteration
Improving a result in small rounds rather than one big request.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to learn prompt engineering?

Not as a special skill. Clear writing, good examples and careful checking cover most of it.

Should I be polite to Claude?

Tone does not hurt, but clarity matters far more than politeness.

Longer prompts or shorter?

As long as needed to be clear, and no longer.

Key takeaways

  • Be specific about audience, purpose and format
  • Ask for a plan, then work in short rounds
  • Check the output and save what worked as standing instructions
