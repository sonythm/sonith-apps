Risk management basics

Why the first question is how much could be lost, and how position size, stop limits, drawdown, diversification and pre-order checks work. General education only.

Reading level

Most people start by asking what a position might gain. Risk management starts from the opposite end: what is the most this could cost, and could I live with that? This part explains the main ideas in plain language, each followed by an Expert view. All numbers are illustrative and nothing here is advice.

Start with the loss, not the gain

In plain words

Think of crossing a river on stones. Before stepping, you ask what happens if a stone wobbles. A gain is optional; a loss that is too large can end the game. Many risk textbooks therefore treat survival as the first aim. This is a principle of thinking, not an instruction to do anything.

Expert viewWhy the loss comes first

A gain has no fixed limit in principle, but the possible loss can be known in advance for some products and can exceed the amount put in for others. Risk is usually described with a maximum loss (the worst case), an expected loss under assumptions, and a tail (rare, large events). Estimates rest on models, and models can be wrong, so the maximum loss is the most solid number to know.

Position sizing: how big is one position

In plain words

Position sizing means choosing how much to put into one position so that a bad outcome stays small. In the picture, a position is sized so that a failure costs about one percent of capital. With a smaller risk fraction the size shrinks; with a larger one it grows.

Real example

Illustrative only: capital 10,000 units, risk fraction 1 percent, so 100 units at risk. If one unit would lose 5 units when the plan fails, the size is 100 divided by 5, which is 20 units. Twenty failures in a row, each costing 1 percent of the then-current capital, would leave about 82 percent of the starting amount.

Expert viewSizing formulas in words

Fixed fractional: size equals capital times the risk fraction, divided by the loss per unit if the plan fails. Fixed amount: the same money at risk each time, regardless of capital. Volatility-scaled: size is inversely proportional to a measure of how much the price typically moves, so quieter things get larger positions and wilder things smaller ones. Kelly-style formulas relate size to an assumed edge and odds; they depend on inputs that cannot be known precisely and are very sensitive to errors, so practitioners often discuss using only a fraction of the result. These are descriptions of concepts, not recommendations.

Stop limits: an exit agreed in advance

In plain words

A stop limit is a pre-agreed exit point, like deciding before a hike to turn back at noon whatever the view looks like. The limits are real: if the price gaps past the level, the exit happens at the next available price, which can be worse. A stop is a tool, not a promise.

Expert viewGaps, slippage and stop types

A stop order becomes a market order when the trigger level is touched, so the fill can be well beyond the trigger when prices jump or liquidity is thin. A stop-limit order sets a worst acceptable price, but then may not fill at all. Neither can protect against a market that is closed overnight. The distance between the trigger and the actual fill is slippage, and it tends to be largest exactly when markets move fastest.

Daily loss limit: a circuit breaker

In plain words

Houses have a fuse that cuts power before wires overheat. A maximum daily loss is the same idea: a pre-agreed figure that, once reached, stops new activity for the day. It prevents one bad day from turning into a spiral of rushed decisions.

In plain words

Take 100 units. After a 50 percent fall you hold 50. A 50 percent rise on 50 gives only 75. To get back to 100 you need to double: a 100 percent rise. The deeper the fall, the steeper the climb back, which is why limiting the depth of falls matters.

Expert viewDrawdown maths

If the fall is d (as a fraction), the rise r needed to recover satisfies (1 minus d) times (1 plus r) equals 1, so r equals d divided by (1 minus d). Maximum drawdown is the largest peak-to-trough fall in a period; drawdown duration is how long it takes to regain the peak, and can matter as much as depth. Consecutive percentage changes multiply, they do not add, which is why a 10 percent loss followed by a 10 percent gain does not return to the start.

Diversification, and where it stops helping

In plain words

Spreading across different things means one failure does not decide the whole outcome. The catch is that things that seem unrelated in calm times can move together in a crisis, so the benefit is real but not unlimited.

Expert viewCorrelation in stress

Diversification works to the extent that the parts are not perfectly correlated. Correlation is measured from past data and is not constant: it often rises in market stress, so the diversification benefit measured in calm periods can shrink when it is most needed. Concentration risk is the opposite problem: one position, sector, counterparty or idea making up a large share of the total, so a single event has an outsized effect. Hidden concentration (different positions driven by the same underlying factor) is common.

Leverage multiplies risk

Real example

Illustrative only: with exposure ten times capital, a 10 percent adverse move equals the whole capital. Gains and losses are both scaled by ten. Some products can lose more than was put in.

Why risk checks come before orders

In plain words

A gate before the road means nothing passes unless it meets the rules. If checks ran after an order, they could only describe damage. The rule is simple: the order cannot exist until every check says yes.

Expert viewPre-trade versus post-trade checks

Pre-trade checks are deterministic tests applied to a proposed order (size, price band, limits, duplicates) and can reject it. Post-trade checks monitor what actually happened (positions, exposure, unexpected fills) and raise alarms or trigger reductions. Both are needed: pre-trade stops known problems, post-trade catches the surprises the first set did not foresee.

Operational risks and a kill switch

In plain words

A kill switch is a big red button that stops new activity and, if configured, reduces what is open. It is useful because in a surprise nobody wants to hunt for the right menu. When the situation is unclear, the safe default is to do nothing new.

Expert viewRisk of ruin intuition

Risk of ruin is the chance that losses reach a level from which recovery is practically impossible. Intuitively it grows with larger position sizes, with higher variability of outcomes, and with a lower chance of favourable outcomes; it falls when each position is small relative to capital. It is a modelling concept: real markets violate the neat assumptions behind simple formulas, so treat any quoted figure as rough.

The first question is how much could be lost, not how much could be gained.

Frequently asked questions

Does a stop limit cap the loss?

Not strictly. Gaps and slippage mean the exit can be at a worse price than the level.

Why does recovery need a bigger percentage than the fall?

Because the percentage is applied to a smaller amount after the fall.

Does diversification remove risk?

No. It can reduce some kinds of risk, but things can move together in stress.

Is a small risk fraction a recommendation?

No. The numbers here are illustrative to show the arithmetic.

Careful

Educational content, not investment advice. Trading, especially with leverage and options, can lose more than you expect.
