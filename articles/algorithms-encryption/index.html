Encryption and digital signatures, the basics

How locks and keys explain encryption, why there are two families (shared-key and public-key), what digital signatures prove, and how HTTPS combines them. Concepts only, no recipes.

Reading level

Encryption is the part of computing where algorithms protect secrets. It is also the part where good intentions go most badly wrong, because a design that looks clever can be broken by someone who knows the field. This article explains the ideas with locks and keys, so you can read about security with understanding. It is deliberately about concepts: it does not teach you to build anything, and it should not be used that way.

Careful

Safety first: use vetted libraries and standard protocols. Never implement cryptography yourself, and do not copy key-handling code you cannot explain. For which methods and settings are current, use current standards from a trusted source.

Locks, keys and three words

In plain words

Imagine a letter in a locked box. The letter is the plaintext. The locked box is the ciphertext. The key is what opens it. Anyone can see the box, but without the key the letter stays unreadable.

Expert viewKerckhoffs's principle

A long-standing rule in the field, known as Kerckhoffs's principle, says a system should stay secure even if everything about it is public except the key. In practice this means the method is published and examined by many experts, and only the key is secret. Secret, home-made methods ("security through obscurity") have a poor record, because the people who most need to examine them never get the chance.

Symmetric encryption: one shared key

In plain words

Symmetric encryption is a box with one key, and you and a friend each hold a copy. It is quick. The hard part is getting your friend a copy of the key without anyone else copying it on the way. This is the key-sharing problem.

Expert viewAuthenticated encryption, in words

Hiding data is not enough: an attacker who cannot read a message might still change it. Modern designs therefore use authenticated encryption, which both hides the data and attaches a check so that any tampering is detected when the message is opened. This is one more reason to use a well-tested library mode and not assemble pieces by hand: the order and combination of the pieces matters, and mistakes are easy to make and hard to see.

Public-key encryption: a lock anyone can close

In plain words

Picture a box of open padlocks that you hand out freely. Anyone can lock a box with one of your padlocks, but only you have the key that opens them. You never had to meet the sender in advance.

Expert viewWhy not use it for everything?

Public-key methods rest on mathematical problems believed to be hard to reverse, and they are much slower than symmetric methods. So they are typically used for small jobs: agreeing on a shared key, or protecting a short key, and for signatures. Standards in this area evolve, including work on methods designed for a future where much larger computers might exist. Follow current guidance from a trusted standards body and do not rely on summaries like this one for choices.

Hybrid: both together

The slow method is used briefly, to set up a secret that two sides share. The fast method then does the heavy lifting. Nearly every secure connection you use is built this way.

Hashing: a fingerprint, not a lock

Hashing was covered in the hashing part of this series. Here it matters for two reasons: it detects changes to data, and it is the right tool for storing passwords, because a service should never need to read your password back.

Digital signatures: proof of who and what

In plain words

A signature works like a wax seal that only you can press, which everyone can recognise. If the seal is intact, the letter came from you and was not opened and altered on the way.

Expert viewWhat a signature does and does not give

In outline, the signer computes a hash of the message and uses the private key to produce a signature over it. A verifier recomputes the hash and uses the public key to check the signature. Two properties follow: authenticity (it was made by the holder of the private key) and integrity (the message has not changed since signing). A signature does not hide the message, since signing and encrypting are separate operations, and it only proves that the key was used, not that the key's owner is who you assume. That last gap is why certificates exist.

HTTPS in one picture

Expert viewForward secrecy, in words

Some key agreement methods use fresh, temporary values for each connection. The benefit, called forward secrecy, is that if a long-term key is stolen later, old recorded conversations still cannot be unlocked with it. Current standards for secure connections favour designs with this property.

Certificates and authorities

In plain words

A public key alone is just a number. How do you know it belongs to the real shop and not an impostor? A certificate is like an identity card: it says "this key belongs to this name", and a trusted body, a certificate authority, has signed it. Your browser and device come with a list of authorities they trust.

Expert viewTrust is a chain

Authorities can sign certificates for other authorities, forming a chain from the site up to a trusted root. A certificate is checked for a valid signature, a matching name and whether it has expired or been revoked. The weakness is human and organisational: you are trusting the authorities, so the system's safety depends on their care and on the software's checks.

Common mistakes

Also avoid hard-coded keys (they leak with the source code) and storing passwords unhashed (keep only slow, salted hashes).

Expert viewKey management is the hard part

In most real incidents the mathematics held and the handling failed: keys were left in code repositories, shared by email, never rotated or kept next to the data they protected. Good practice means generating keys with trusted tools, storing them in purpose-built secret stores or hardware, limiting who can use them, rotating them, and planning for loss. Treat key handling as the main design problem, not an afterthought.

What encryption does not protect

Expert viewLimits worth remembering

Encryption protects data from people who cannot get the key. It does not hide the fact that a conversation happened, it does not help when an attacker controls the device that holds the decrypted data, and it does not fix tricking a person into giving access away. Security is a chain of such links, and a strong cipher is only one of them.

Try it by hand: a toy shift

Careful

The Caesar shift below is a toy for understanding the words plaintext, ciphertext and key. It is not secure and must never protect anything real.

Pick the key 3 and move each letter three places along the alphabet, wrapping from Z to A. The word CAT becomes FDW. To decrypt, shift back by 3.

That last step is the lesson: a method with few possible keys fails at once to trial and error, and clever tricks can break far bigger designs. This is why experts design, review and publish the real methods.

Everything can be public except the key, so protect the key, and never invent the lock.

Glossary
Plaintext
The readable data before encryption.
Ciphertext
The scrambled data after encryption.
Key
The secret value that locks or unlocks data.
Symmetric encryption
One shared secret key does both jobs.
Public-key encryption
A public key locks, a private key unlocks.
Digital signature
Proof made with a private key that a message came from its holder and is unchanged.
Hash
A fixed-size fingerprint of data, not reversible.
Certificate
A signed statement tying a public key to a name.
Certificate authority
A trusted body that signs certificates.

Frequently asked questions

Is a signature the same as encryption?

No. A signature proves who made a message and that it is unchanged; it does not hide the message.

Is hashing a way to hide passwords?

Hashing is used to store passwords without keeping them, but it must be a slow, salted method from a trusted library, not a quick hash.

Can I write my own encryption if I keep it secret?

No. Secret designs are the weakest. Use vetted libraries and standard protocols.

Does the padlock in the browser mean a site is safe?

It means the connection is encrypted and the site proved its name. It does not mean the site's owner is trustworthy.

Key takeaways

  • Encryption hides data with a key
  • signatures prove who sent it and that it was not changed
  • Shared-key encryption is fast, public-key encryption solves sharing keys, and real systems use both
  • Never build your own cryptography: use vetted libraries and standard protocols
