Agile in the age of AI: shorter cycles, same discipline

Do we still follow agile when AI writes much of the code? Yes, but compressed: shorter cycles, sharper briefs, stricter verification, and lessons fed back into the team's instruction files.

  • Keep agile, but compress the cycle to days, not weeks
  • Building shrinks while deciding and verifying grow
  • Feed every lesson back into your written instructions

Reading level

A fair question I hear: if an AI can produce a working feature in an afternoon, do we still need agile? My answer, from building small apps with AI help, is yes: keep agile and compress it. The ideas (small steps, real feedback, honest status, one person deciding priorities) matter more when code is cheap to produce, because it is also cheap to produce the wrong thing. This article is general and personal, not a claim about any company or survey. Each section has a plain picture and an Expert view.

Careful

Labels used. Proven foundation marks widely used, long-established practice (the Agile Manifesto, the Scrum Guide, common delivery research). My adaptation marks my own practice with AI, such as two to three day cycles, which is not an industry standard and which I cannot back with independent evidence. Try it small and keep what helps.

The short answer

In plain words

Think of a restaurant kitchen testing a new dish. It does not cook for a month before anyone tastes it. It makes a small batch, serves it to a few real diners, watches their faces, and adjusts. AI makes cooking faster; it does not make tasting optional.

Proven foundation

The Agile Manifesto's four value statements are: individuals and interactions over processes and tools; working software over comprehensive documentation; customer collaboration over contract negotiation; responding to change over following a plan (checked at agilemanifesto.org on 2026-10-21). Short feedback loops, ordered priorities and honest status are mainstream practice.

My adaptation (not an industry standard)

The claim that AI shifts the effort from building to deciding and verifying, and that cycles can therefore be compressed to a few days, is my own reasoning and experience, not an established finding.

Expert viewAgile is a set of values, not a calendar

The Agile Manifesto's values (working software, responding to change, customer collaboration, individuals and interactions) say nothing about two-week sprints; the sprint is one popular implementation. What compresses with AI is the build step. What does not compress is deciding what to build, checking that it is correct and safe, and learning from real use. So the sensible move is shorter timeboxes with the same feedback discipline, not dropping the discipline.

What changes and what stays

Where the time goes

The bottleneck moved: building got cheap, so judgement and checking became the work.

Expert viewWhy the bottleneck moves

When one stage speeds up, the next constraint dominates, which is the idea behind the theory of constraints. If generation is fast, the queue forms at review and testing, and a large unreviewed change is a risk, not an achievement. Teams that ignore this tend to measure output (lines produced) and quietly accumulate defects. Measure flow and outcomes instead: how quickly a small change reaches users safely, and how often changes need fixing afterwards.

The short cycle

My adaptation (not an industry standard)

The 30-minute brief, two to three day cycles and a 15-minute retro that ends with a written change to the instruction file are my own adaptation of agile for AI-assisted work. They are not an industry standard. For a fuller playbook with the board, planning ritual and gates, see Planning short delivery cycles in the AI era and the roles, meetings, quality gates and release.

Expert viewCadence options

Scrum can be compressed (short sprints with the same events shortened to match). Kanban with explicit work-in-progress limits suits AI-assisted work well because it keeps the number of open changes reviewable. A shape-up style approach sets an appetite (how much time the idea is worth) and a small team builds within it. Pick one, write it down, and keep the feedback step. For a solo developer, a light Kanban board with a limit of two or three items in flight and a fixed weekly review is usually enough.

A good brief

The brief is where the human adds the most value. A vague brief gives the AI room to invent; a precise one gives you something to check against.

Text
BRIEF
Outcome: A user can mark a bill as paid from the bill screen.
Acceptance checks:
  - Tapping Paid moves the bill to the Paid list.
  - Undo restores it within 10 seconds.
  - Nothing is sent off the phone.
Out of scope: reminders, editing amounts.
Constraints: keep the existing data format; follow current code style.
Examples: a bill paid twice by mistake; a bill with no due date.
First: read the relevant files, then give me your plan and questions. Wait for my OK.
Expert viewBriefs as testable specifications

Good acceptance checks read like tests. Write them so they could be turned into automated checks, and ask the AI to propose tests first. Examples help because they pin down edge cases (empty input, duplicates, boundaries) that models otherwise skip. Keep a brief short enough to review in a minute; if it takes a page, the slice is too big.

Done means verified

Review effort scaled to change size

Expert viewSmall changes, trunk-based habits

Small, frequent merges to the main branch (trunk-based development) keep each review manageable and make problems easy to locate. Pair that with feature flags (hide unfinished work behind a switch) and staged rollout (release to a small group, then widen) so that shipping small does not mean exposing everyone to risk. A useful rule: if you cannot explain a change in two sentences or review it in ten minutes, it is too large to merge.

Guardrails that matter more with AI

Real example

In my own app work, short cycles are backed by scripted smoke tests, random-event testing on an emulator, and a staged release to a small test group. Once a sample-data slip was caught before release; the fix was not just correcting the data, but adding a line to the written instructions so the same slip would not be repeated.

Expert viewTest strategy as the safety net

The test pyramid (many fast unit tests, fewer integration tests, a handful of end-to-end tests) is a good default. Two techniques suit generated code especially well: contract tests (check that components honour the agreed interface) and mutation testing (deliberately break the code to confirm the tests notice). Watch for tests that merely restate the implementation, and for an agent that makes failing tests pass by weakening them. Read the tests, not only the green tick.

Instruction files are team process

The most valuable output of a retro is a better written instruction. Each lesson becomes a line in the project's instruction file or a reusable skill, so the next cycle starts smarter. See CLAUDE.md and memory, Skills explained, and Reuse context and share it with your team.

What can go wrong

Careful

Watch for these risks: over-trusting AI output, huge unreviewed diffs, scope creep, skipping user feedback, and "vibe coding" without tests. Any one of them can turn speed into rework.

Expert viewSecurity and parallel work

Generated code needs the same security review as any other code, and a little more suspicion: check dependencies the AI suggests, input handling, authentication and secrets, and any new network calls. Running several agents in parallel can be productive but creates merge conflicts and review overload; limit parallelism to what you can review, and give each agent a separate slice and branch. There are two working modes: pair mode (you steer step by step) for unclear or risky work, and delegated mode (the agent works from a good brief and you review the result) for well-defined slices.

A sample weekly rhythm for a solo developer or small team

DayWhat happensOutput
MondayPick the next slices, write briefs (30 minutes each)Two or three briefs
Tuesday and WednesdayBuild, verify, ship one small slice to the test groupA released slice
ThursdayBuild and verify the next slice, read feedbackA second slice, feedback notes
Friday15-minute retro, update instruction files, tidy the backlogBetter instructions, a clean backlog
Expert viewMeasuring without fooling yourself

DORA, a long-running software delivery research programme, defines these delivery metrics (checked at dora.dev on 2026-10-21): change lead time (the time for a change to go from committed to version control to deployed in production), deployment frequency (how many deployments over a period), failed deployment recovery time (how long it takes to recover from a deployment that fails and needs immediate intervention), change fail rate (the ratio of deployments that need immediate intervention) and deployment rework rate (the ratio of unplanned deployments that happen because of a production incident). They work best as trends within your own team, used to learn, not as targets to hit. I will not quote benchmark numbers here because I cannot verify them.

Faster building is only an advantage if verifying and learning keep up.

Glossary
Agile
A way of working in small steps with frequent feedback and the freedom to change direction.
Sprint
A fixed, short period of work, often one to four weeks.
Brief
A short written description of an outcome, checks and limits for a piece of work.
WIP limit
A cap on how many items may be in progress at once.
Trunk-based development
Merging small changes to the main branch often.
Feature flag
A switch that hides unfinished work from users.
Staged rollout
Releasing to a small group first, then widening.
Test pyramid
Many fast tests at the bottom, few slow ones at the top.
Appetite
How much time an idea is worth, set before building.

Frequently asked questions

So is Scrum dead?

No. Its ideas still work. What changes is how long each loop takes and how much effort goes into briefs and verification.

Should AI write the tests too?

It can draft them, and often does well, but a human should read them and make sure they test real behaviour.

Does this only work for solo developers?

No. Teams add shared instruction files, small pull requests and review habits, and the same loop applies.

Where do I start?

Write one good brief for one small slice, verify it properly, ship it to a few people, and hold a 15-minute retro.

Key takeaways

  • Keep agile, but compress the cycle to days, not weeks
  • Building shrinks while deciding and verifying grow
  • Feed every lesson back into your written instructions
