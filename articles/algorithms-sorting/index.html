Reading level

Sorting is one of the oldest jobs in computing: put names in alphabetical order, prices from low to high, messages newest first. It is also the best way to meet the central idea of algorithms: there are many ways to do the same job, and they differ enormously in how much work they need. This part shows four classic sorts as animated bars. Each section has a plain picture and an Expert view with the precise costs. If you prefer no motion, every animation has a text version of its steps underneath.

The job, and why it matters

In plain words

Think of a hand of playing cards. Sorting is arranging them from lowest to highest. Different people do it differently: some swap neighbours, some pick up cards one at a time, some split the pile in two. Those are exactly the four methods in this article.

Expert viewWhat "sorted" and "cost" mean

Sorting orders items by a key under some comparison rule. Algorithms are compared by how many basic steps they need as the list grows to length n, written in Big-O notation (part 1). Comparison-based sorts (all four here) cannot beat about n log n comparisons in the worst case; that is a proven lower bound for sorting by comparing items. Beyond speed, two properties matter: stability (items with equal keys keep their original order) and extra memory (whether the method needs a second array).

Bubble sort: swap neighbours

In plain words

Walk along the row comparing two neighbours. If the left one is bigger, swap them. After one walk, the biggest bar has bubbled to the end. Repeat for the rest.

Expert viewBubble sort in numbers

Pseudo-code: repeat n-1 passes; in each pass compare each adjacent pair and swap if out of order; each pass fixes one more element at the end. Worst and average case take about n squared / 2 comparisons, so time is O(n squared); best case O(n) if you stop when a pass makes no swaps. Extra memory is O(1) and it is stable. It is rarely used in practice because other simple methods do the same job with fewer operations; its value is teaching.

Insertion sort: build a sorted hand

In plain words

Pick up cards one at a time and slide each into the right place in the hand you already hold. The hand on the left is always sorted.

Expert viewWhy insertion sort still matters

Time is O(n squared) in the worst and average cases, but O(n) when the data is already nearly sorted, because each new item moves only a short way. It is stable, in place (O(1) extra memory) and has very little overhead. That makes it a good choice for small lists and for finishing off nearly sorted data, and many practical sorting routines are known to switch to an insertion-style step for small pieces. I am describing the general idea here and not asserting what any particular library does.

Merge sort: split, then merge

In plain words

Cut the pile in half, again and again, until every pile is one card. Then merge piles in pairs: look at the top card of each pile and always take the smaller. Merging two sorted piles is quick and easy.

Expert viewDivide and conquer in practice

Merge sort splits the list into halves (log n levels) and merges at each level in linear time, giving O(n log n) in the best, average and worst cases. Merging needs a temporary array, so extra space is O(n). It is stable when ties are taken from the left half first, and its predictable running time makes it a common choice for sorting records and for sorting data too large for memory (external sorting merges sorted chunks from disk).

Quick sort: pick a pivot

In plain words

Pick one card as the pivot. Put everything smaller on its left and everything bigger on its right. The pivot is now in its final place. Do the same to the left and right piles.

Expert viewPivots and worst cases

Quick sort averages O(n log n) with small constant factors and sorts in place (about O(log n) stack space), which is why it is fast in practice. Its worst case is O(n squared), which happens with consistently bad pivots (for example always taking the last item of an already sorted list). Common defences are choosing a pivot at random, taking the median of a few items, or switching strategy if recursion goes too deep. The usual in-place versions are not stable.

Stability, in plain words

Expert viewWhen stability matters

If you sort a table by one column and then by another, a stable sort preserves the first ordering among ties, which is how multi-key sorting is often built. If you need stability but have an unstable method, you can add the original position as a tie-breaker key.

Which should you use?

Careful

In real programs, use your language's built-in sort. It is well tested and tuned. Writing your own sort is for learning, not for production.

Try it by hand

Different methods, same result: the difference is how much work they do.

Frequently asked questions

Why learn sorting algorithms if libraries already sort?

They teach how to reason about cost, trade-offs and design, which applies far beyond sorting.

Which sort is fastest?

It depends on the data and the limits. Quick sort ideas are fast on average, merge sort is steady, insertion sort wins on tiny or nearly sorted lists.

Can anything beat n log n?

Not by comparing items alone. Special methods (such as counting items by value) can be faster when keys are small whole numbers, but they use different tricks.

Key takeaways

  • Simple sorts are easy but slow on big lists
  • Merge and quick sort split the work to be much faster
  • Stability and memory use matter as much as speed
