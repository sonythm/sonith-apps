Options explained for beginners

What calls and puts are, how strike, expiry and premium fit together, what an option is worth at expiry, and why buyers and sellers face very different risks. Illustrative numbers only.

Reading level

An option is a contract. It is one of the most misunderstood tools in trading, partly because the words sound technical and partly because the payoffs are lopsided. This part explains options with everyday pictures first, then gives an Expert view with the precise terms. Every number here is illustrative: invented round figures chosen to make the arithmetic easy, not a description of any real market.

The idea: a right, not a duty

In plain words

Imagine you like a house priced at 100 and you are not ready to commit. You pay the owner a small, non-refundable fee of 5 to reserve the right to buy it at 100 any time in the next three months. If the house becomes more valuable, you use your right. If not, you walk away and lose only the fee. The owner keeps the fee either way. That fee is the premium, and the reservation is a call option.

An option has three ingredients you will see everywhere.

Expert viewFormal definitions

An option is a derivative: its value depends on the price of an underlying (a share, an index and so on). A call gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy the underlying at the strike on or before expiry (depending on style). A put gives the right to sell for the strike. The buyer (holder, long) pays the premium and has the right. The seller (writer, short) receives the premium and takes the obligation if the buyer uses the right. Options are usually traded in standard contracts, each covering a fixed quantity of the underlying (the contract size or lot), so one contract means many units, and the payoff scales with that quantity.

Calls and puts

In plain words

A put is like an insurance policy. You pay a premium to be covered if the value of your car drops below an agreed amount. If nothing bad happens, the premium is gone, the same as an insurance fee that was never claimed. It is not wasted by design: it paid for the protection during that period.

Whether an option is worth using at a given moment has a name.

For a put it is the other way round: a put with strike 100 is in the money when the share is below 100.

Expert viewIntrinsic value and time value

The premium has two parts. Intrinsic value is what the option would be worth if used right now: for a call, the underlying price minus the strike, or zero if that is negative; for a put, the strike minus the underlying price, or zero. Time value is the rest of the premium: what the market pays for the chance that the option becomes more valuable before expiry. An out-of-the-money option has no intrinsic value, so its whole premium is time value. At expiry time value is zero, so the option is worth only its intrinsic value.

What happens at expiry

Take a share priced at 100, strike 100 and premium 5 (all illustrative, ignoring costs and the contract size). The payoff at expiry depends only on where the share ends up. Payoff here means the buyer's or seller's gain or loss on the option itself, premium included.

Careful

Look at the seller's column. The seller's gain is capped at the premium received (5), while the loss keeps growing. For a seller of a call there is no upper limit on how high the share could go, so the loss can be many times the premium. For a seller of a put the loss is large but bounded, because a share cannot fall below zero. Sellers are usually required to put up a deposit (margin) and can be asked for more.

Expert viewReading payoff shapes

At expiry the payoff of a long call is max(S - K, 0) minus the premium; of a long put, max(K - S, 0) minus the premium (S the underlying price, K the strike). These are kinked, not straight lines, which is why options are called non-linear: a share has a constant payoff slope, while an option's slope changes at the strike. Short positions mirror them. Before expiry the curve is smoother, because time value remains. These tables ignore trading costs, taxes, the time value of money and early exercise.

Why time value fades

In plain words

A ticket to an event next month has more chance of being useful, or resold, than a ticket for tonight when you are not going. As the date nears, the "maybe" shrinks. An option works the same way: with a long time left, much can happen; with a day left, little can. So even if nothing moves, the time part of the premium drains away. This drain is called time decay.

Decay works for the seller and against the buyer, but it does not remove the seller's risk: a large move can outweigh many days of decay.

Expert viewDecay is not linear

Time value does not fall in a straight line. For an at-the-money option it tends to fall faster as expiry nears, roughly with the square root of the time left in simple models. The measure of this is called theta, covered in the next part, which also covers how implied volatility affects the premium.

Using and settling an option

When a buyer exercises, a seller is chosen to meet the obligation. This is called assignment. Some contracts settle by delivering the underlying; others settle in cash, paying the difference between the underlying price and the strike.

Expert viewEuropean versus American

A European option can be exercised only at expiry; an American option can be exercised at any time up to expiry. The names say nothing about where they trade. Early exercise adds flexibility and can affect pricing, and many options are automatically exercised at expiry if they are in the money by some set amount, depending on the contract rules. Contract specifications, including size, style and settlement type, are published by the venue, so read them rather than assuming.

Myths worth dropping

A buyer pays for a right and can lose only the premium. A seller takes an obligation and can lose far more.

Glossary
Option
A contract giving one side a right and the other an obligation, based on an underlying.
Call
The right to buy for the strike.
Put
The right to sell for the strike.
Strike price
The agreed price in the contract.
Premium
The price of the option, paid by the buyer to the seller.
Intrinsic value
What the option would be worth if used right now.
Time value
The part of the premium above intrinsic value.
Exercise
Using the right in the contract.
Assignment
A seller being chosen to meet the obligation.
Underlying
The asset the option is based on.

Frequently asked questions

Is a bought option always cheaper to lose than a share?

The loss is capped at the premium, which can be the whole amount paid. A share can lose less in a small move, so cheaper in absolute terms is not the same as lower risk in percentage terms.

Why do sellers need a deposit?

Because the obligation can lead to a loss larger than the premium, the seller must show they can meet it.

Does an option have to be exercised?

No. A buyer can close it out, exercise it or let it expire.

Are the numbers here real?

No. All figures are illustrative round numbers to show the arithmetic.

Careful

Educational content, not investment advice. Trading, especially with leverage and options, can lose more than you expect.

Key takeaways

  • A buyer's loss is limited to the premium, a seller's loss can be much larger
