Shares, futures and options compared

What you own or owe with a share, a futures contract and an option, how leverage and expiry change the risk, and why derivatives exist at all. Illustrative examples only.

Reading level

Shares, futures and options are often mentioned in one breath, but they are different kinds of thing. One is a piece of ownership, one is a binding promise, and one is a right that can be bought or an obligation that can be taken on. This part compares them side by side, with plain pictures first and an Expert view after each. All numbers are illustrative.

Three different things

In plain words

A share is like owning a brick in a shop wall: you hold something real. A futures contract is like signing a promise to take delivery of goods next month at today's agreed price: you hold a duty. An option is like paying a small fee for a reservation: you hold a right you may or may not use, and the person who took your fee holds the duty.

Expert viewUnderlying and derivative

A share is the underlying asset in this comparison. Futures and options are derivatives: their value is derived from an underlying asset, index or rate. A futures contract is an obligation for both sides to trade at an agreed price on a set date (some settle by delivery, others by paying the cash difference). An option gives the buyer a right and the seller an obligation. Both are standardised contracts with fixed size and expiry dates set by the venue where they trade.

The maximum loss, stated plainly

Expert viewWhy a limit is not the same as low risk

A limited loss does not mean a small or unlikely loss. An option buyer who pays a premium of 5 on a share priced at 100 (illustrative) risks 5, but that is the entire amount paid, and options near expiry can lose most of their value without the underlying moving much. Meanwhile a futures position has no premium: every unit of adverse price movement is a loss, and because only a deposit was paid, the loss can pass the deposit and the holder must cover the gap.

Leverage and margin

In plain words

Leverage is a long lever on a small weight: a small push at one end moves a lot at the other. The lever works in both directions. A small adverse move can wipe out a large share of the money put up.

Expert viewMargin in more detail

Initial margin is the deposit to open a position; maintenance margin is the minimum to keep it open. Rules vary by contract and by broker, and may be raised when markets become unsettled, so a position can be asked for more money precisely when moves are large. Leverage is the ratio of contract value to deposit. Losses are measured on the full contract value, not the deposit, which is why a 10 percent move against a tenfold-leveraged position can equal the entire deposit.

Why derivatives exist

In plain words

Two people can have opposite worries. A farmer worries the price of grain will fall before harvest. A baker worries it will rise before the next order. If they agree a price today for delivery later, each swaps an uncertain future for a known price. That is hedging: moving risk to someone who wants it.

The same contract can be used by someone who has no grain at all and simply bets on the direction of the price. That is speculation. It adds liquidity to the market, but it carries the full risk of the contract with no offsetting business to cushion it.

Expert viewRisk transfer is not risk removal

A hedge trades one risk for another. The farmer gives up the chance of a higher price, and the hedge can lose money on paper even while the farm sells at a good price. Basis risk (the hedge not tracking the exposure exactly) and the cost of margin calls on the hedging side are real trade-offs. Derivatives markets exist because of this transfer: they let those who want to reduce a risk deal with those who accept it, usually for a price, and the sums accepted by one side are the exposures shed by the other.

A worked comparison (illustrative)

Suppose a share is priced at 100 and then falls to 90. Here is what each position holds, ignoring costs.

PositionAmount put upLoss after the fall to 90
Own one share10010 (a tenth of the amount put up)
Futures-style position on one unit, deposit of 101010 (the entire deposit)
Option bought, call, premium 555 (the entire premium)

The same fall in the underlying uses up a very different share of the money put up. A fall to 80 would take the futures-style loss to 20, which is twice the deposit. The option buyer's loss would still be 5.

Costs and complexity

Who should not use leverage without understanding it

This is a plain risk statement, not a recommendation. Leverage can produce losses larger than the money first put up. Anyone who cannot afford a loss of the whole deposit, who does not understand how margin calls work, or who has not read the contract specifications, faces a risk of losses they did not expect. Read the official risk warnings published in your own country before using leverage.

A share is ownership, a future is a duty, an option is a right for one side and a duty for the other.

Frequently asked questions

Is an option the same as a future?

No. A futures contract binds both sides; an option binds only the seller.

Can a share lose more than I paid?

Held outright, a share can fall to zero, so the loss is limited to the amount paid. Selling a share short is different and can lose more.

Why does a futures position have a deposit?

It is a good-faith amount held against losses; it is not the price of the contract.

Are these numbers real?

No. They are illustrative round figures.

Careful

Educational content, not investment advice. Trading, especially with leverage and options, can lose more than you expect.

  • Derivatives exist mainly to move risk between people
