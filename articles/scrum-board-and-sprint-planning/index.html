The Scrum board and sprint planning, explained

What a good Scrum board looks like, how a card is written, who does what, how to plan a sprint step by step, and how to size work in plain words, with a worked example for a small team.

Reading level

Two questions I hear often: what should a Scrum board look like, and how do we plan a sprint? This article answers both in plain language, with a picture first and an Expert view in each section. The facts about Scrum itself (roles, events, time-boxes, artifacts) come from the official Scrum Guide at scrumguides.org, checked on 2026-10-21. Board layouts, story points and similar practices are widely used habits, not parts of the Guide, and I say so where it matters. For how all this changes when AI helps write the code, see Agile in the age of AI.

The order rail in a kitchen

In plain words

In a busy kitchen, order slips hang on a rail: new orders, being cooked, ready to serve. Anyone can glance at the rail and know what is happening, what is stuck, and how many orders the cooks can handle at once. A Scrum board is that rail for a team's work.

Expert viewWhat the Scrum Guide does and does not say

The Scrum Guide defines a Product Backlog (committed to a Product Goal), a Sprint Backlog (committed to a Sprint Goal) and an Increment (committed to a Definition of Done). It does not prescribe board columns, story points, swimlanes or burndown charts; teams adopt these because they make the three artifacts visible. A board is a view of the Sprint Backlog, so it should show the sprint goal and the Definition of Done, and everything on it should be work the team actually plans to finish in the sprint.

What a good board shows

Expert viewColumns, WIP limits and swimlanes

A work-in-progress (WIP) limit on the in-progress column forces the team to finish before starting; when the limit is reached, people help finish rather than pick up new items. An in-review column is worth having especially when review is a real bottleneck, which it often is with AI-assisted work. Swimlanes group cards by story so you see each story's slices move together, but too many lanes hide unrelated work; use them only when they help the team discuss the sprint goal.

Expert viewSplitting stories well

A common checklist for good stories is INVEST: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small and Testable (a long-standing practice, not part of the Scrum Guide). Split by vertical slices, a thin piece that goes through every layer and delivers something a user can notice, rather than by layers ("build the database", then "build the screen"). A story that cannot be finished in a few days is usually two stories. Many teams also keep a Definition of Ready for items entering a sprint (clear, small, sized, acceptance criteria written); the Definition of Done, in contrast, is in the Scrum Guide.

Expert viewRefinement is not an event

The Scrum Guide describes Product Backlog refinement as an ongoing activity that adds detail, order and size to items, not as one of the five events. Teams usually hold regular refinement sessions so that the top of the backlog is ready for planning. For shorter sprints the time-boxes shrink in proportion; a two-week team that aims for planning in 60 to 90 minutes is well within the maximum, which is a limit, not a target.

How to plan a sprint

Expert viewSprint goal craft, and running planning in 60 to 90 minutes

The Guide describes three planning topics: why the sprint is valuable (the goal), what can be done, and how the chosen work will get done. A good goal is one sentence about an outcome ("customers can pay a bill from the bill screen"), not a list of tickets, and it gives the team flexibility about the work needed. To keep planning short: refine beforehand so items arrive small and clear; start from the goal, then capacity; pick the first few items together and size the rest quickly; stop when the plan is credible, not exhaustive; and write tasks only for the first days, since the plan is adapted daily.

Sizing in plain words

In plain words

Sizing is comparing, not measuring: this card is about the same effort as that one, and twice as much as the small one. Relative sizes are quicker and more honest than guessing hours.

Expert viewPoints, velocity and honesty

The Scrum Guide says that the Developers who will do the work are responsible for the sizing, and it does not mandate any scale. Story points and t-shirt sizes are team conventions. Velocity (the sum of points finished per sprint) is best read as a range over several sprints, and it is a planning aid for the team, not a performance measure for individuals. If points are used to compare teams or push people, they get inflated and stop being useful. Splitting an item small enough that sizes barely matter is often better than debating points.

Capacity: a worked example

Capacity is how much time the team really has this sprint, after leave, meetings and unplanned work.

Real example

A team of three, working two-week sprints, expects about 16 person-days of real capacity. Its sprint goal is "customers can pay a bill from the bill screen". It picks four items that look like about 11 points, since its last few sprints finished between 10 and 14. It keeps some days free for review, fixes and the unexpected, and breaks the first two items into tasks of under a day each.

Expert viewUnplanned work, spillover and capacity honesty

Unplanned work is normal; reserve a share of capacity for it (many teams hold back around a fifth to a third, a rule of thumb rather than a standard). If the sprint goal is at risk, the Developers talk to the Product Owner about the scope, rather than quietly working late. Items not finished return to the Product Backlog and are re-ordered, not automatically carried over; count how often it happens as a signal about planning, not as blame. With AI help, reserve review time explicitly, because generation is quick and verification is where the sprint is won or lost.

Board variants

BoardBest forNotes
Scrum boardA team working in sprintsShows the sprint goal and a fixed set of items, reset each sprint
Kanban boardContinuous flow, support workNo sprints; limits on work in progress; items flow as they arrive
Personal boardOne person or a solo developerThree columns (next, doing, done), a limit of two or three items

Widely used tools include Jira, Azure DevOps, GitHub Projects and Trello, and a wall of sticky notes works just as well. The tool matters less than whether people look at the board every day.

Metrics to watch

Expert viewUsing metrics without gaming them

Use metrics to ask questions, not to grade people. A burndown that drops in big steps often means items are too large; a long cycle time often points to a review queue; high carry-over suggests that sizing or capacity assumptions are off. I am deliberately not quoting benchmark numbers, because good values depend on the team and the work. Inspect the trend in the retrospective and change one thing at a time.

Copy this: sprint planning checklist

Text
SPRINT PLANNING CHECKLIST
[ ] Product goal and backlog order are clear (Product Owner)
[ ] Top items are refined: small, clear, with acceptance checks
[ ] Sprint goal written in one sentence
[ ] Capacity worked out from real availability (leave, meetings, buffer)
[ ] Items picked to fit the goal and the capacity
[ ] First items broken into tasks of under a day
[ ] Definition of Done visible on the board
[ ] Review time reserved, especially if AI helps write code
[ ] Team agrees the plan (a commitment to the goal, not to every ticket)

Remote team tips

Keep the board in one shared place and open it in the daily check; use short written updates for people in different time zones; hold planning in two shorter sessions if needed; and make blocked flags visible so help can find them.

A board is a conversation made visible. If nobody talks about it, it is wallpaper.

Glossary
Sprint
A fixed-length period, one month or less, in which the team works toward a sprint goal.
Product Backlog
The ordered list of everything the product may need.
Sprint Backlog
The items chosen for the sprint plus the plan to deliver them.
Increment
A finished, usable step toward the product goal.
Definition of Done
The shared description of what finished means.
WIP limit
A cap on how many items may be in progress at once.
Story points
A relative size for work, agreed by the team.
Velocity
How much a team usually finishes in a sprint, best read as a range.
Burndown
A chart of remaining work over the sprint.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a sprint be?

The Scrum Guide says one month or less, with a fixed length. Many teams use one or two weeks, and AI-assisted teams often use shorter cycles inside the sprint.

Do we have to use story points?

No. The Guide does not require them. Some teams use t-shirt sizes or count items instead.

Who decides what goes into a sprint?

The Product Owner orders the backlog; the Developers decide how much they can do and plan the sprint together with the Product Owner.

Can the sprint plan change?

The goal stays fixed; the plan is adapted daily as the team learns more.

Key takeaways

  • A board shows the work, the limits and the goal on one page
  • Plan from a sprint goal and your real capacity, not from wishes
  • Points are for conversation, not promises
