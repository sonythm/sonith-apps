Common mistakes and myths

Picture cards for common myths, plain explanations of over-leverage, emotional trading, overfitting and other biases, scam red flags, and habits that are education rather than advice.

Reading level

Many costly mistakes in markets are not technical. They come from beliefs that feel sensible and are wrong. This part lists the common myths as picture cards, explains the mistakes behind them in plain words, and adds an Expert view with the proper names. It is consumer education, not advice.

Six myths, one picture each

Expert viewWhy the last myth matters

Frequency of being right and size of outcomes are two separate numbers. Suppose, illustratively, that a plan is right 9 times in 10, gaining 1 unit each time and losing 20 units on the tenth. Per ten tries that is 9 gained and 20 lost: a net loss despite being right 90 percent of the time. Looking at hit rate alone hides this; the payoff of each outcome matters as much as how often it occurs.

Over-leverage

In plain words

Over-leverage means taking a position so large relative to your capital that an ordinary move against you hurts badly. Like driving close behind another car at speed: it works until the moment it does not.

Real example

Illustrative only: with exposure ten times capital, a 10 percent move against the position equals the whole capital. With exposure two times capital, the same move costs 20 percent. The market did the same thing; the size of the position decided the damage.

Expert viewLeverage and forced exits

Leveraged positions often come with a margin requirement: if losses reduce the cushion below a threshold, the position can be reduced or closed automatically, sometimes at an unfavourable price and at the worst moment. This turns a temporary fall into a permanent loss, because there is no time left to wait. Some products can lose more than the amount initially put in.

Emotions after a loss

In plain words

Revenge trading means decisions made to win back a loss quickly, driven by feelings rather than a plan. It tends to raise the stake and lower the care, which is the opposite of what risk management asks for.

Why does it happen? After a loss, the mind wants to restore the old number, and waiting feels like accepting the loss. Raising the stake seems like the quickest route back. But the market does not know what you lost, and a larger stake on the next attempt only makes the next loss larger too. Many people find that a pause, a written rule about stopping for the day, or a limit set in advance helps more than willpower in the moment. This is an observation about behaviour, not a prescription.

Overtrading and costs

In plain words

Every action usually costs something: fees, the gap between buy and sell prices, and slippage. Acting more often means paying more often, so costs can quietly swallow results. Ignoring costs is one of the most common beginner errors.

Expert viewWhy costs compound

Costs are paid whether the outcome is good or bad, so they act as a steady drag that must be overcome before anything is gained. They also reduce the capital that later positions work with, and a percentage cost applied repeatedly compounds like a negative growth rate. Spread and slippage widen in fast or thin markets, so real costs are often higher exactly when activity is highest.

Fooling ourselves: biases in one line each

In plain words

Overfitting is memorising last year's exam. You can score full marks on that paper and still fail next year's, because you learned the answers, not the subject. A plan tuned until it fits old data perfectly may just have memorised noise.

Expert viewOverfitting and out-of-sample testing

Flexible rules with many adjustable settings can fit any history. The standard defence is out-of-sample testing: tune on one period, then test once on data that was kept aside and never used for tuning. Walk-forward testing repeats this over rolling periods. The multiple-testing problem is that if many variations are tried, some will look good by chance alone, so the more things tried, the less any single good-looking result means. Statisticians adjust for this, and the honest summary is that a historical result is evidence of what happened, not a measure of what will happen.

Expert viewBehavioural biases, named properly

Confirmation bias: favouring evidence that supports a prior belief. Loss aversion: losses feeling larger than equal gains. Overconfidence: overrating the accuracy of one's own judgement. Sunk-cost fallacy: continuing because of what is already spent. Gambler's fallacy: thinking a run of outcomes makes the opposite more likely when the events are independent. Recency bias: giving too much weight to recent events. Naming a bias does not remove it, but written rules decided in advance reduce its room to act.

A short word on two of these. Look-ahead bias often sneaks into tests by accident: a figure that is only published at the end of the day gets used as if it were known in the morning, and the test looks wonderful for a reason that could never happen live. Survivorship bias appears whenever failures quietly disappear from a list, so that what remains looks better than the whole group was.

Scam red flags

In plain words

If someone promises outcomes, rushes you, asks you to keep it secret or asks you to send money to a personal account, treat these as warning signs. A genuine opportunity survives a few days of checking; a scam usually cannot.

Expert viewWhy scams work

Scams rely on well-known persuasion levers: urgency (act now), authority (an impressive title or screenshot), social proof (others are in), and secrecy (do not tell anyone). Fabricated screenshots and invented track records are cheap to produce, so a displayed result proves nothing. Consumer-protection bodies generally advise checking who is responsible for a service through official channels, never paying into a private account, and taking time before any transfer. If something has already gone wrong, the usual guidance is to stop sending money and report it to the relevant local authority.

Healthy habits (education, not advice)

These are ideas that appear again and again in consumer education. They do not tell anyone what to do with their own money; they describe how careful people tend to think. Records help you see your own patterns instead of your memories of them. Knowing the maximum loss before taking part turns a vague worry into a number. Understanding the product means being able to explain how it pays out in good and bad cases.

Certainty is the one thing no market, expert or tip can offer.

Glossary
Leverage
Exposure larger than the capital put in.
Revenge trading
Emotional decisions to win back a loss.
Overtrading
Acting more often than a plan needs, paying costs each time.
Slippage
The difference between expected and actual price.
Overfitting
Tuning a plan until it fits old data too closely.
Look-ahead bias
Using information not yet available at the time.
Survivorship bias
Counting only what survived.
Out-of-sample
Data kept aside and not used for tuning.

Frequently asked questions

Is a good past result a sign of a good plan?

Not by itself. It may be overfitting or luck, which is why testing on unseen data matters.

Why do costs matter so much?

They are paid every time and repeat, so they steadily reduce results.

How can I spot a scam?

Look for promised outcomes, pressure, secrecy and requests to pay into private accounts.

Are the habits listed advice?

No. They are commonly described principles, presented for education.

Careful

Educational content, not investment advice. Trading, especially with leverage and options, can lose more than you expect.
